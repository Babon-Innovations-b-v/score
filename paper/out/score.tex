\title{SCORE: A Framework for Synthesizing Complete, Owned, Responsive
Environments}
\author{J.P. Kardolus, Babon Innovations B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands}
\date{Working draft, 21 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Games, films and robot training need 3D environments that are complete,
editable and owned by their makers; world models and one-shot generators
give frames no engine opens, or one fused scene without separate objects
or clear rights. Studios already build them by a standard: scenes
layered in OpenUSD, each scene sitting within its parent scene and each
department's work on it a layer of its own that can be checked and
changed apart from the rest. SCORE builds environments by this
convention. The creator decides from the top down: he locks an
environment concept (EC), its style and its physics, which holds the
scenes and their layering, and then a concept for each scene, in order
of parent scene and child scene. The framework makes every layer below:
it reads each locked concept into a scene inventory and a plan, and
builds the scene's layers with open vision foundation models, procedural
tools and coding agents, each layer checked before the next is made.
Every model allows commercial use of its output, so the creator stays
the author. We describe the framework with its first environment, 26
scenes derived from the game 2099, as the worked example, and separate
what its runs prove, what is built but has not run, and what is missing:
the first run brought every scene through its completion gate, but its
video was not accepted, and the remake from new designs has not run.
Code and paper are available at
https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT license).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Interactive 3D environments are the raw material of games, film and
embodied AI {[}1{]}, and building one is expensive: every object is
modelled, surfaced, placed, given collision, lit and given sound in one
style, and the environment stays editable. Generative models promise to
take over much of this, but in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game
workers thought generative AI was hurting the industry {[}2{]}; a tool
for building environments has to leave the result in the creator's
hands.

Studios already have a standard for building environments: OpenUSD
{[}3{]}, the scene description Pixar made for its films. In it each
artist works in a file of their own, a layer, and the layers are stacked
in a stated order, so artists in different departments work on one scene
without editing each other's work; and scenes are assembled from smaller
ones by reference {[}3{]}. In this convention a scene sits within its
parent scene and holds its child scenes, and each department's work on
it (layout, set dressing, surfaces, light, effects, motion, sound) is a
layer that can be checked and changed apart from the rest. Decisions
come from the top down: the look of the whole first, then each scene's,
and the departments make the layers below. SCORE builds environments by
this convention, with the creator deciding and the framework doing the
departments' work. The creator locks an environment concept (EC): its
style and its physics, what everything stands on and in and how it
moves. The EC holds the scenes and their layering. For each scene a
concept is then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene, each
child scene drawn to the shape its parent scene's plan gives it. From
each locked concept the framework reads a scene inventory and writes a
plan, and makes every layer below with open vision foundation models
{[}4{]}, procedural tools and coding agents, each layer its own OpenUSD
layer, checked before the next is made. One runner keys, checks and
accepts every layer, and paid work runs in batches the creator buys from
an estimate (Section 2).

World models generate an environment as frames {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} that no
engine can open. 3D environment generators {[}7{]}, {[}8{]} produce
geometry, but mostly as one fused asset, from which separate objects are
recovered after the fact {[}9{]}, {[}10{]}. Both work in one shot, with
no layers a creator could open, so a creator cannot find or change the
step behind a fault. Procedural systems {[}11{]}, {[}12{]} and coding
agents that write scenes as programs {[}13{]}, {[}14{]}, {[}15{]},
{[}16{]} keep the environment explicit, but rebuild a scene that already
exists or draw their objects from a fixed library, and such agents judge
their own geometry unreliably {[}17{]}. Open vision foundation models
{[}4{]} now read shapes {[}18{]}, masks {[}19{]}, depth {[}20{]} and
parts {[}21{]} out of pictures, and a coding agent can connect them as
tools; none of them provides one style across objects that different
models made, which the field names as an open problem {[}22{]},
{[}23{]}.

Recent agents write Blender programs and check them against a target:
from one image, in stages of geometry, materials, composition and light
{[}24{]}; from text or video, as worlds for physical reasoning {[}25{]};
and with motion added {[}26{]}, {[}27{]}. Benchmarks find that their
procedural models often float or come apart {[}28{]}, and that on
dynamic scenes rebuilt from video the best model scores 0.91 on static
scenes but 0.67 on dynamic ones; mesh quality does not track fidelity,
and VLM judges agree with people in 89.3 percent of comparisons but near
chance between close models {[}29{]}. In SCORE motion is a layer of its
own, checked like the others, which this gap between static and dynamic
scenes supports.

SCORE's environments are meant to be \textbf{complete}: every object,
surface, light and sound exists wherever the viewer looks. They are
\textbf{owned}: every model in the framework allows commercial use of
its output, so the creator stays the author, holding the full commercial
rights. They are meant to be \textbf{responsive}: every object has real
collision, mass and material. And they can be any kind of
\textbf{environment}, from a house on a plot of ground to a system of
worlds. To our knowledge, no other openly available environment
generation work joins vision foundation models into engine-ready
environments that are built from reference pictures, steered by a
person's choices at each step, and made only with models that allow
commercial use of their output.

We contribute \textbf{the SCORE framework}, which builds an environment
by the studios' convention: the creator decides the EC and each scene's
concept from the top down, and the framework makes every layer below as
an OpenUSD layer of its own (Section 2); \textbf{measured gates} for
every layer of a scene and a completion gate over them (Section 3); and
\textbf{evidence from a first environment}, which separates what its
runs prove from what is built but has not run and what is missing
(Section 4).

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

An environment is a table of stages; environment 1's has 149 rows, 89 of
them one for each person's look, dress or take. A \textbf{node} is one
stage for one scene (\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the environment,
and one runner makes, judges and accepts every node. The creator works
in the creator cockpit, one signed-in web page: there he designs each
scene (Section 2.2), buys paid work as batches and reviews what the
runner reports. From an approved design everything runs without a
further yes.

\subsection{Keys, gates and accepts}\label{keys-gates-and-accepts}

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record.
The key hashes the stage and scene, a code version, the contracts of its
output and inputs, its parameters, a seed derived from the
environment's, the pinned model images, every environment file it reads
and the accepted output of every node it reads. The code version hashes
every source file the stage imports, leaving out the runner and the
renting code, which change what a run costs, not what it makes. A fault
fixed in one stage moves only its key and those after it. Outputs are
kept by content hash, never overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown,
with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown
blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate
passed and it is no worse than the one accepted before: no answer
dropped, no measure moved the wrong way by a step a viewer could see, no
gate missing. Later nodes read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps
four actions, buttons in the cockpit applied on his own PC: reject an
accepted output (it moves back to the one before, and every node that
read it is stale), accept a worse one with a reason, waive one gate for
one key, and override the runner for one paid run. When the creator
accepts a worse output, that accept records what was allowed, and a
later output no worse than the one taken is accepted with that accept
named. None is needed for a run; a barrier keeps agents from them (a
store that refuses the creator's records from an agent's process, and a
hook).

\textbf{Batches.} Paid work is bought as a batch. Before it starts, the
runner gives one estimate and one ETA from measured unit costs (Section
3.7); the creator's yes to that estimate is the only spend decision, and
the batch's stop is its own high estimate plus 10 percent. Inside a
batch every paid job runs once, with no retry; its checks only sort each
output into a success or a named failure, and a failure becomes a batch
gap that the line carries to the completion gate instead of stopping.
Spending more on a gap is a new batch and a new yes. A paid node is
cleared only when every gate on its inputs passed, the month's books are
known from the providers' bills, and its batch's stop has room for its
forecast; everything that rents a machine or calls a paid model refuses
without that clearance, and buying again more than EUR 5 of accepted
outputs needs a written reason. Each node's review page is built from
the store alone, and the creator reviews a scene in a 3D viewer on the
store's copy of its accepted assembly.

\textbf{The creator steers: the ship.} Figure 1 shows the creator's
control on one piece of environment 1, the ship, in its first run. The
creator's picks were found pictures of a shuttle, a one-deck cutaway, a
cockpit and crew quarters; we do not show them. From them an agent
planned the bridge alone (v1, a 4.6 by 4.6 m room of 27 pieces). The
creator corrected the plan in words: a smaller cockpit, the rooms
environment 1 needs, a long flat wedge rather than a rocket, a nose less
pointed, and an outline measured from drawings rather than shaped from
words. Each correction made a new plan, and the creator accepted the
seventh. Every plan is ours and cost nothing; the two drawings the
outline was traced from cost EUR 0.28 and are not shown. The ship's
shape follows found artwork the creator chose to use: shuttle concept by
PAUL (zana), found online.

\subsection{From concept to scene}\label{from-concept-to-scene}

\textbf{The design.} Before any paid batch the creator decides each
scene's design: its concept and its plan. He first locks an environment
concept (EC), in a short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro {[}30{]} in the
cockpit: its style and its \textbf{physics}, with his reference
pictures. The EC holds the scenes and their layering, its top scenes and
the child scenes each may hold. Its physics is what everything stands on
and in and how it moves, at the scale he sets: a plot of ground with a
gravity and a sun path, one world, or a system of bodies that move one
another, a real body taking its values from a cited table and an
invented one given by its radius, mass, orbit and spin. Each thing in it
takes the form its scale calls for: a body near enough to matter moves
in the physics simulation, a far source of light is a light, and what is
too far to move is a backdrop. An environment plan, written by the plan
writer below, places each top scene's site on its physics' ground at
true size and works out each site's sky at its scene time. For each
scene a concept is then locked, in order of parent scene and child
scene, drawn on a concept review page, each draw one Nano Banana Pro
{[}31{]} call that the creator's click pays for, where he marks areas
and asks for a new draw. Every new draw is sent a \textbf{blockout}
first: a grey model built in code from its parent's plan at real sizes
and rendered free from the fixed concept view, so the draw keeps its
layout and proportions and adds the look. A child scene is drawn only
once its parent scene is picked and planned, on a crop of the parent
scene's concept and its own shell inside the parent's, so child scenes
fit the parent scene they stand in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} The creator's pick is the approval. It
starts the scene inventory reading: Gemini 3.1 Pro lists every object of
the picked concept as a row with its kind, count, anchor and size in
metres, judged against the scene's outline, and SAM 3 {[}19{]} boxes
each row on the concept; a row without metres, or a disagreement between
the two, is a named gap. One more Gemini 3.1 Pro call writes the plan in
metres, element by element, as environment 1's hand-written plans were:
outline, walls, openings with their swing, levels, stairs, roofs,
terrain heights, each item's shape and a section. The SAM 3 boxes only
order the items, since boxes from a perspective painting laid flat put
objects in the wrong place. Free checks follow, each failure a gap said
where it is: every row placed as often as it counts, inside the outline,
no two floor items in each other, every door and standing spot reached
on foot, and the child scenes tiling their parent scene. On the plan
review page the creator moves, resizes and corrects, and his edits redo
what follows. Readings and plan writings run without a yes under the
creator's standing allowance, their cost shown in the cockpit. Only a
scene with a design gets the rows of a batch.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route is one loop, the same at every scale. A
scene starts as its big shape, the parent scene, with a concept, a plan
and a rough model: the concept goes through picture to model whole, and
the rough model gives the plan its proportions. The parent scene's plan
is then split into the shapes of its child scenes (rooms, corridors,
holds), each sized inside it. Once those shapes are fixed, every scene's
concept, rough model, close-ups and finishes are made in parallel, since
from then on each is a matter of proportions inside a fixed shape;
zooming into a child scene repeats the same steps. This is the creator's
order of 19 October 2026. The runner has its two rows:
\texttt{rough-model} puts a scene's concept through Pixal3D whole after
its plan, and \texttt{shell-split}, free, cuts a parent scene's plan
into its child scenes' shapes, gated on \texttt{tiled}, \texttt{reached}
and \texttt{bounded}, which each child scene's plan reads. Neither has
run on environment 1: no rough model has been made, and no plan of it
has a split yet.

\textbf{Plan and scene record.} Nothing is made or placed that is not an
inventory row. The plan stage is gated on \texttt{mapped} (every element
a row, a door or a written drop; every door says where it leads). The
scene record is written from the plan, inventory, made ground and sky,
gated on \texttt{game-free} (the record cites nothing from the game the
environment came from), \texttt{plan-matched} (the shell within 5 cm of
the plan), \texttt{openings} (every doorway onto a floor, no view into
nothing) and \texttt{seed-matched} (every old entry matched or dropped
with a reason). The \texttt{game-free} gate sees cited numbers only. In
environment 1's first run a coding agent wrote the inventories and
plans, and in 18 of its scenes the data still took positions from the
source game (Section 4).

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every scene is one OpenUSD stage {[}3{]};
each object, one per real-world object, carries its kind, transform,
collision and each part's library surface. Its layers lie in a fixed
order: a slot for the creator's edit layer on top, then people, sound,
light, motion, effects, crops, machines, settle, ground and the
generated base. The edit layer exists only as that slot, an empty layer
the exporter makes once and never writes again; a test on a two-box
scene shows an edit there surviving a regenerated base, but the runner's
assembly does not yet carry a creator's edit from one assembly to the
next. Each scene is assembled once, from every layer's accepted output;
its \texttt{layers} gate answers unknown while a needed layer has none,
and an old output never stands in.

\textbf{Models, generation first.} Every object goes the prop route
first: Nano Banana Pro draws its close-up once from its crop of the
concept, Pixal3D {[}18{]} builds it, the surface stage paints it, and a
likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with its close-up. A
close-up that fails the shape check's measured half (a plain border, one
object filling the frame, the row's proportions) is a batch gap, so
Pixal3D is never paid for it. Only an object whose checks fail is built
by a code builder a coding agent writes, reversing the earlier parts
check (the creator's choice, 12 October 2026). On 13 of the hub's
objects both orders end the same (Table 1, Figure 2), but generation
first draws a close-up of every object. Every close-up of eight thin and
flat objects failed the shape check, so code builds carry them. The
hatch wheel shows the gain: its code build's outline overlap was 0.45, a
fail, and its generated model's 0.60.

\section{Stages and their gates}\label{stages-and-their-gates}

\subsection{Surfaces: the finish route}\label{surfaces-the-finish-route}

Everything a scene shows comes from the creator's library, score\_lib,
or is a written exception: every model, kit, finish, effect, light,
sound, character, ground and algorithm (the creator's rule of 18 October
2026). Models give shape only, picture-made and code-built alike, so one
style holds across them: a picture-made model keeps its geometry and its
finish is replaced, never kept as the picture's own texture, its
baked-in colour or a default grey. Every part takes one surface from a
shared library of rule-based surfaces, written as ProcFunc functions
{[}32{]} on Infinigen's shaders {[}11{]}, coloured from the scene's
palette and worn only where wear has a cause (Appendix D).

The finish route gives each model its finishes (the creator's choice of
20 October 2026). Free renders of the raw model cover every face from as
many angles as a coverage check needs, and each view is split into
colour regions of the model's own texture. One Gemini 3.1 Pro answer a
model, a scene's models in one batch, lists its finishes, each matched
first to a library surface with its settings (palette token, roughness,
metal, seed), and labels each region of each view. Before anything is
baked, a free check compares each region with its label's surface and
makes a model whose colours lie more than ΔE 32 off a gap; the labels
then vote across views into one finish a face, and a face no label
reaches or whose views disagree is a gap too. Each part is painted from
its library surface and baked in one paid job, and one batch recheck
against the close-up sorts each model into a pass or the named gap ``no
library surface fits''. This route replaced the finish-map route of
environment 1's first run, in which Nano Banana Pro drew the close-up
again with one flat colour per finish, up to three times, and SegviGen
{[}33{]} split the mesh along that map; that route gave every piece its
own rust and stains until the parts were painted from the library
(Appendix A). The finish route has run its free views on two scenes; its
paid steps have not run.

The bake is checked against the close-up. A finish's surface must lie
within ΔE 32 of its colour there, else a nearer surface of its family is
taken or a new one is proposed to the creator, and the part fails by
name. From the greenhouse's water tank, which the creator found baked as
three near greys and tipped, came two checks: a bake fails when it
darkens a finish the close-up shows lighter, or turns all surfaces into
one grey; and short legs are stretched onto one level plane, a foot over
3 cm off the floor failing. The bake's finish borders follow the model's
real edges, as the cut-down copy it bakes onto is shaded smooth except
at folds of 60 degrees or more.

A written exception names the scene, the object, what it is, why it is
not from the library, who allowed it and when; only the creator allows
one, and one without a reason or an owner refuses the whole file. A free
library check traces every material, model, emitter, light, sound source
and character of a scene's accepted assembly to a library entry, by
content hash or a recorded origin, and the cockpit shows what does not
trace as the scene's problems. It is not a gate of the completion gate,
since wiring it in would move the keys of paid rows; instead the library
rows at the end of every line, which hand each accepted output to the
library, fail when an output is covered by no entry or written
exception, so nothing reaches the library untraced. Each new entry then
gets a preview clip, rendered in the batch with the films' renderer.

\subsection{Resting}\label{resting}

Loose objects are dropped onto the made ground in a physics simulation;
one that would turn more than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm is a
resting fault of the layout and keeps its laid pose. The settle stage
lays where each object comes to rest as a layer (held objects snapped
onto their holders, a stack onto one support, an object that would rest
worse kept as laid), gated on \texttt{rested} and \texttt{resting} (no
fault that appears with the layer and not without it).

\subsection{Light}\label{light}

Lamp strengths are measured, never copied from the source game. The
light layer places live lamps from the scene record, with the sky and
its bodies. One paid render per scene gives every lamp its own Cycles
light group on the scene's views; because light adds up, the look at any
strengths is mixed from these passes without rendering again, and a free
sweep chooses the scale that brings the scene's brightness within 1.5
times its concept's, halving any lamp that turns what it lights white.
Outdoors the suns are swept after the lamps. A lamp whose fixture stops
90 percent of its rays moves out of it, and one behind a clear lens
stands just outside, as Cycles passes no light through a glowing lens.

\subsection{Motion, effects and sound}\label{motion-effects-and-sound}

\textbf{Motion.} Each moving thing (a path, a hinge, a spin, a rail, a
turn, a climb) has its kind's speed range cited from real machines,
gated on \texttt{clear} (no moving thing within 3 cm of a still thing or
another moving thing, read every quarter second), \texttt{speeds},
\texttt{loops}, \texttt{grounded} and \texttt{covered} (every still prim
of a moving kind says why). \textbf{Effects} are emitters measured
against real footage (photographs of lift-offs, rover films of Martian
dust devils), gated on \texttt{attached}, \texttt{footage},
\texttt{light} (the flame lights the pad at least twice as brightly as
the scene's lamps) and \texttt{outside} (no open-sky particle inside a
room). \textbf{Sound:} every source a scene names (room tones, objects,
motions, each surface's steps) gets the environment's picked file or one
MOSS-SoundEffect {[}34{]} take of its brief, gated on \texttt{sources},
\texttt{level}, \texttt{likeness} (CLAP {[}35{]} score at least 0.30,
the tenth percentile of the environment's picks; a take under it is a
gap) and \texttt{no\ freesound}.

\subsection{People}\label{people}

People are made like props: a FLUX.2 klein {[}36{]} close-up in SOMA-X's
bind pose, built by Pixal3D, painted by the surface line, over SOMA-X's
body fitted just inside (Gauss-Newton, 24 shape numbers), whose skin
weights each look point takes from its closest triangle; Kimodo's clips
{[}37{]}, from sentences, move it. The \textbf{shell gate} passes a look
when 99 percent of its soft points lie within 5 cm of the body, its
worst point recorded. Hard parts ride one joint, belts are cloth, each
clip gets its own verdict (tucks, hard parts stretched, cloth or body
through cloth) and casts use only passing clips; a far-only look is
judged on its far body (Appendix B). \textbf{Casts:} SAM 3 finds the
concept's figures, a judge tells people from pictures, reflections and
empty suits, and casts are gated on \texttt{counted}, \texttt{standing},
\texttt{clear}, \texttt{made} and \texttt{drapes}; no crowd copy fills a
tenth of a view's height.

\subsection{Completion}\label{completion}

A scene is complete when its gate passes on its final assembly: every
row made or dropped with a reason, nothing unmade, no placeholder or
resting fault, the creator's picks shown, every layer and shown scene
present, nothing cited from the source game. \textbf{Accepted gaps.} A
fault the creator decides to ship with is written down as an accepted
gap: who decided, when, why, the objects and their measured faults. It
moves only those objects' faults from the gate where they arise to the
completion gate, which fails each gap by name with the numbers: a scene
whose accepted gaps are its only fail is complete apart from accepted
gaps, every gap listed with who decided it; any other fault leaves it
not complete, and the gate is never loosened. A batch gap moves the same
way, the creator's yes to the batch being its decision, and is listed
with the batch run that made it. A hook stops an agent claiming a scene
is done while its gate fails: agents judging their own scenes agree with
a measured geometry score only 45.8 percent of the time {[}17{]}.

\subsection{Prices and ledgers}\label{prices-and-ledgers}

Machines and Google calls write ledger lines with their node's
clearance; the month's spend is the higher of ledgers and bill, which
caught misses: EUR 1,554 billed against EUR 1,387 in the cloud ledger on
12 October 2026, and EUR 549 billed by Google for a day the earlier
ledger put at EUR 30. Over 13.8 hours of environment 1's first run the
ledgers hold EUR 117.45 of machines and EUR 34.19 of Google calls, most
for painting (EUR 37.61), the finish maps (EUR 26.59), the people's
bodies (EUR 16.09) and the light passes (EUR 10.17).

\textbf{Prices.} Every paid stage is priced from a unit cost measured on
the ledgers (a close-up EUR 0.07 from 135 calls; a Pixal3D model EUR
0.11 likely and 0.20 high from 52 jobs; a preview clip EUR 0.16 from one
run of six), times the units its forecast rule counts, times the bill's
share over the ledger. A stage with no measured run has no price: its
estimate is shown apart as a guess and never added to a price. The
finish route's list and recheck are priced from the earlier route's 275
list calls at the batch tier's half price, not yet from the route
itself. The batch estimate and the runner use the same unit prices, and
on 21 October they agreed to the cent per stage and per model on every
designed scene whose line could start. Before, the runner priced a run
from a flat figure in its table, which overstated work it could carry
over: the four runs of 15 October were forecast at up to EUR 72.57, 33,
70.50 and 24 and cost 10.13, 10.14, 0.67 and 0.60, while measured unit
costs held (22 close-ups for the ship estimated at EUR 3.10 to 6.20,
actual 3.50; seven box checks 1.75, actual 2.19; its eight shell kits
1.20, actual 0.60). One ship run spent EUR 13.58 before it stopped on
missing free inputs; since then every input is checked free before a
paid run.

\section{Evidence}\label{evidence}

Environment 1 is the worked example: 26 scenes, from a city square and a
flat to a Moon base, a ship and Mars, derived from the game 2099. Its
first run ended on 18 October; its remake from new designs has not run.
Table 3 sorts the framework's parts into what these runs prove, what is
built but has not run, and what is missing.

\textbf{The first run.} Each scene was filmed once its completion gate
passed or failed only on its accepted gaps: a 2 s title card, then one
walk of 4 s (96 frames at 24 fps) on a path the planner keeps clear of
walls, objects and people, with the scene's own sound. The film gate
measures each film (its size, a sound track that is not silent, no black
or frozen stretch after the title card, its length and the clearance)
and the runner accepts it; no person reviewed the films before the cut,
and the coding agent that made the cut checked only its order, frames
and sound. On 18 October the cut held all 27 films, 26 scenes and the
ship's hull, 3 min 9 s (Figure 3). The final completion gate, run again
on every scene on 17 and 18 October, left all 26 scenes complete apart
from accepted gaps (2 to 19 each) and none complete; many of those gaps
were decided by the overseer, the coding-agent session that guards the
run under the creator's rules, and wait for the creator's confirmation.
The creator watched the video on 18 October and did not accept it,
naming five faults: the ship looks poor, weakest where his own direction
stopped; the films cut too fast, about 3.5 s a scene; no people appear;
the sound is only ambient, with no voices or event sounds; and moving
things do not move convincingly. On 19 October the disk holding the
store, both spend ledgers and the work folder was lost; the code, the
cloud bucket and the library's entry records survived, and the store is
rebuilt only by today's keys and gates, so most of the run's outputs
must be made again.

\section{Limitations, future work and
conclusion}\label{limitations-future-work-and-conclusion}

One creator judges, and he has not yet accepted a run of environment 1.
Closed models (Nano Banana Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro) draw the concepts and
close-ups and read and plan every scene; they may change or go. Much of
the first run was lost with its disk, and the new design step, the
finish route, the shapes-first loop and the code bodies have not yet
carried a scene through a batch; people are not usable, light is live,
objects are not interactive, and no game engine opens the result yet.
Next: the remake's batch, the shapes-first loop on the ship, people, an
engine adapter with physics and interaction, an edit layer the assembly
carries, three more environments, open models, LEGO-Bench {[}17{]}. In
short, SCORE keeps, keys and judges every stage's output, so a fault is
fixed where it happens; an accepted environment will show if it holds.

Three findings shaped the method. First, deterministic checks catch what
agents miss: in round one, the share of rays leaking out through walls
and roof fell from 1.53 percent of 5,817 to zero, and the colour noise
within surfaces from a median of 31.1 to 3.2 percent, but only walking
through the loaded room showed 26 old models still in it, which became
the made-only check. Second, generated pieces that kept their pictures'
colours made the room inconsistent, so surfaces come only from the
library. Third, generated composite objects melted their parts together,
and generated boxes leaned (one locker by 13 degrees), which led to one
model per real-world object and to the straightness check. By round six
the room used 0.45 million triangles, against 1.96 million in round
four, and 187 MB on disk, against 487 MB.

\section{Check details}\label{check-details}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Room checks} cast rays to find light leaking out through walls
  and roof, find pieces in front of a real opening and roof and wall
  fittings more than 3 cm off their surface or turned more than 5
  degrees from it, and count pieces, triangles and lamps against the
  room's budget.
\item
  \textbf{Door check:} every door must stand in a wall or partition that
  fully separates its two sides, tested by walking a grid from one side
  to the other.
\item
  \textbf{Model check:} every model must have walls at least 3 mm thick.
  A generated box fails if one of its sides tilts more than 2 degrees or
  is warped. Making a generated model solid stops if its bounding box
  grows more than 3 percent.
\item
  \textbf{Likeness gate:} a made model's outline from eight sides must
  overlap its close-up's by at least 0.50, a least set on the hub's
  twelve accepted generated models with a margin of 0.05, and its DINOv2
  {[}4{]} similarity must reach a least set the same way; without that
  calibration the gate answers unknown.
\item
  \textbf{Finish maps (the first run's route):} a close-up's map passes
  when its outline overlaps the close-up's by at least 0.85 and it uses
  every listed finish; a part whose second finish holds 20 percent of
  its voted area mixes finishes and fails.
\item
  \textbf{Bake check:} each baked part within ΔE 20 (CIE76) of its
  surface's colour, worn and dirtied as far as its scene's wear and
  dirt, and within ΔE 32 of its close-up's; lightness order fails when
  the close-up shows one surface more than 10 L* lighter and the bake
  makes it more than 3 L* darker; one look fails when no two
  near-neutral surfaces bake far enough apart for a viewer to tell at
  the scene's view distance, while the close-up shows them more than ΔE
  24 apart. What a viewer tells is measured, not set: Stone, Szafir and
  Setlur measured the CIELAB difference half of their viewers see
  between two colours s degrees wide, per axis ND(50, s) = C + K/s, with
  (C, K) = (5.08, 0.75) for L*, (5.34, 1.54) for a* and (5.35, 2.87) for
  b* on sizes 1/3 to 6 degrees {[}38{]}; two surfaces are told apart
  when their difference, each axis over its ND, is 1 or more. A
  surface's size is its model's middle size times the root of its area
  share, seen from the scene's nearest view. This replaced a fixed ΔE
  12, which failed the wreck's access cover on a 9.3 L* step between its
  steel and its scorched white that its owner sees (11.3 degrees from
  the nearest view; half of viewers see 5.2 L* at that size); a quadrant
  of a standing model's base more than 3 cm off the floor fails. Glass,
  screens, lamps and print are judged by their own colour and left out
  of the order.
\item
  \textbf{Settling and resting check:} an object that would turn more
  than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm when dropped keeps its laid
  pose and is marked as not resting; small debris, with no side over 1.4
  m, may turn freely and drift up to 1 m. Hung objects are exempt by
  their inventory anchor, and fixed objects are not judged for tipping.
  The triage fails a foot off its support and an up axis leaning more
  than 3 degrees.
\item
  \textbf{Light:} the scene's median brightness within 1.5 times its
  concept's and every view within 2.5 times; a lamp is blown when over
  20 percent of the pixels where it gives at least half the light are
  white, and unlit when it gives less than 0.001 percent of every view's
  light.
\item
  \textbf{Motion:} no moving thing within 3 cm of a still thing or
  another moving thing, read every 0.25 s; rounds join within 1 mm;
  speeds within each kind's cited range.
\item
  \textbf{Sound:} CLAP score against the brief at least 0.30, the tenth
  percentile of 55 of the environment's picks; a take no more than 0.5 s
  longer than its brief.
\item
  \textbf{People:} the shell gate passes a look when 99 percent of its
  soft points lie within 5 cm of the fitted body; the worst point and
  its joint are recorded. A clip is usable when no frame tucks more than
  5 mm, no hard part stretches past 1.2 times its bind length, cloth
  crosses cloth no more than in the earlier body's same clip, and no
  part's edges pass through cloth deeper than 3 mm more often than at
  the bind. A far-only look is judged on its joins, likeness and the far
  body's triangles (at most 1,220); the near outfit's triangles and
  shell offset are recorded, not gating. The A-pose outline is judged
  against the finished Pixal3D take the build starts from; the take's
  own loss against the raw model is recorded apart. A crowd copy stands
  only where it fills under a tenth of every view's height.
\item
  \textbf{Worse than accepted:} a measure counts as worse only when it
  moves the wrong way by at least its tolerance, the smallest change a
  viewer could see: one more fault for a count; 0.02 of full scale for
  lightness on 0 to 1 (a halo, fill, beam or haze wash), and 5 of 255
  levels for a colour gap, the step a viewer notices between two
  pictures; 0.05 for a brightness ratio; one percent of the picture for
  a share of it; a twentieth of the Earth's lit disc; 5 mm for a length.
  A score no viewer reads directly, such as a likeness score, is worse
  by any move. Tolerances decide only whether an output waits for the
  creator; a check that fails still fails.
\item
  \textbf{The creator's accept carried forward:} when the creator
  accepts a worse output, that accept records what was allowed, each
  measure's value before and after. A later output that passes every
  gate and is no worse than the output taken, within the tolerances, is
  accepted by the runner with that accept named; a move beyond it still
  waits for the creator.
\item
  \textbf{What the creator acts on:} the creator rejects, accepts or
  waives only an output already seen on its review page. This is the
  creator's rule; the commands do not yet check that the page was
  opened.
\item
  \textbf{People seen by the cameras (experimental):} a clip's crossings
  of body through cloth are counted only where a camera that will show
  the person can see them: the scene's views at the person's cast spot,
  and a close front and three-quarter view. Crossings no camera sees are
  recorded, not counted against the clip. So far this is a measure on
  the stored bodies (Section 3.5); the per-clip gate does not use it
  yet.
\item
  \textbf{Completion gate:} the requirements given in Section 3.6, each
  with its result tied to a fingerprint of the stage's files and inputs;
  a result made on another stage counts as unknown. The placeholder
  check judges every mesh a code builder draws, and only the shell
  builders pass unnamed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{People results in detail (experimental, Section 3.5).} The body
gates are joins, outline, likeness, triangles and shell. Nev's and
Sefa's boot tuck bands lie 9 to 11 mm outside the trouser leg at the
bind pose, in every clip before any motion. Scored on drawn surfaces
only, no clip of any look is usable: skin seams open and settled cloth
crosses itself beyond the bind. Bound as one closed mesh, the player's
limbs sink into chest and hips, 87 to 563 crossing points visible from
the scenes' cameras and close views per clip, the armpit surface
stretching between arm and chest. Where hair or cloth meets the head or
hand skin the two sides are weighted differently and open as the joints
move (cloth seams never open); openings over 10 cm on three looks are
not yet confirmed by a render. Binding the take as one closed mesh
removes the openings. Pushing points out of the look's own volume,
limiting the offset from SOMA-X at the armpits and inner thighs,
fold-centred weights and dual-quaternion skinning each left the visible
crossings the same or higher. A close-up drawn in a wider A-pose (arms
45 degrees below level, feet 35 cm apart) gives a take with fewer
crossings at the bind (the player's 678 against 772) but more in motion,
as the clips bring the arms further down: 10,610 visible crossings
against 4,078 over the player's 19 clips, and no clip clean in either
pose. The clips' joint angles stay within the normal human ranges of the
American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, except hip extension of 24 and
27 degrees (limit 20) in two clips.

\textbf{Decisions recorded in these checks.} Three people's gates are
decisions of the captain, the agent session that then ran environment 1,
taken on 12 and 13 October 2026 under the creator's rules and open to
the creator's reversal. (1) The shell gate counts 99 percent of the soft
points rather than every point, with the worst point recorded: the
points beyond it were loose cloth edges (cuffs, hems, caps), which no
part label names, so they could not be given a joint of their own by
label. (2) A look seen only far away is judged on what is drawn far
away, not on the near outfit it was made from. (3) The A-pose outline is
judged against the finished take, not the raw model, so the take's own
loss is not charged to the build. Earlier, the full-body cut (which
looks get bodies in full and which only far ones) was set by the
people's share of the budget.

\section{The surface library}\label{the-surface-library}

The surface library holds families of variants, among them painted,
steel, aluminium, rubber, plastic, cable, fabric, composite, glass,
screen, light, print, wood, ground, foil, water, skin and hair. After
the hub's second round it held 71 variants made from 14 ProcFunc
recipes; it now holds 369 variants in 26 families made from 22 recipes.
Each variant is a recipe with settings, takes its colours only from the
palette, and is baked into base-colour, metal-roughness and normal maps
at a texture density that depends on how close a viewer can get. Wear
has three levels and comes from causes: edges, and kicks within 32 cm of
the floor that a piece stands on. A surface the finish-map route
proposes enters an append-only registry and joins the library only when
the creator accepts it.

\section{With and without the agent
tools}\label{with-and-without-the-agent-tools}

The tools that check an agent's work were tested on three scenes of
environment 1: a room (the workshop, 814 objects), an outdoor scene (the
street, 1,198 objects) and a dense room (the hangar, 1,657 objects).
They are the completion gate (Section 3.6); a triage of the resting
check, which judges each object by what holds it up, read from its
inventory row, and keeps only real faults {[}59{]}, {[}60{]}; a pick
lock, which refuses an export that would show anything but the model the
creator picked for a row; and a render-and-compare, which lists the
objects least like their close-ups by DINOv2 similarity {[}4{]}. In both
arms, the same agent (Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code, headless) started
from the same exported stage and inventory and was asked to make the
scene as complete as it could in about 50 minutes, through its layout
data only; one arm had the framework from before these tools. Each arm
ran once, so Table E1 shows what happened, not an average.

The tools made building cheaper more often than they made the scene
better. On the workshop and the street, the arm with the tools used
about half the agent's output and cost, and exported the stage once or
twice instead of seven to nine times, because the triage named the few
real faults among hundreds of alarms. On the workshop, 13 of its 18
faults were cleared by writing into the inventory what holds a piece up,
which the creator still has to confirm. On the hangar the arm without
the tools left fewer faults: the arm with them spent its time replacing
five placeholder slabs and trying the drop test, which failed on a room
with no ground mesh (fixed after the run), and the nine faults it left
are models with stray parts below their feet. No agent claimed a scene
was complete, so the gate's hook never had to refuse a claim. No row
carries an owner's pick, so the pick lock was not tested.
