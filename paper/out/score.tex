\title{SCORE: A Framework for Synthesizing Complete, Owned, Responsive
Environments}
\author{J.P. Kardolus, Babon Innovations B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands}
\date{Working draft, 21 October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Games, films and robot training need 3D environments that are complete,
editable and owned by their creators; world models and one-shot
generators give frames no engine opens, or one fused scene without
separate objects or clear rights. Studios already build such
environments by a standard: scenes layered in OpenUSD, each scene
sitting within its parent scene and each department's work on it a layer
of its own that can be checked and changed apart from the rest. SCORE
builds environments by this convention. The creator decides from the top
down: he locks an environment concept (EC), its style and its physics,
which holds the scenes and their layering, and then a concept for each
scene, in order of parent scene and child scene. The framework makes
every layer below with open vision foundation models, procedural tools
and coding agents, each layer checked before the next is made. Every
model allows commercial use of its output, so the creator stays the
author. We describe the framework with its first environment as the
worked example; its results are marked gaps until its remake has run.
Code and paper are available at
https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT license).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Interactive 3D environments are the raw material of games, film and
embodied AI {[}1{]}, and building one is expensive: every object is
modelled, surfaced, placed, given collision, lit and given sound in one
style, and the environment stays editable. Generative models promise to
take over much of this, but in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game
workers thought generative AI was hurting the industry {[}2{]}; a tool
for building environments has to leave the result in the creator's
hands.

Studios already have a standard for this: OpenUSD {[}3{]}, the scene
description Pixar made for its films. Each artist works in a file of
their own, a layer; the layers are stacked in a stated order, so
departments work on one scene without editing each other's work, and
scenes are assembled from smaller ones by reference. In this convention
a scene sits within its parent scene and holds its child scenes, and
each department's work on it (layout, set dressing, surfaces, light,
effects, motion, sound) is a layer that can be checked and changed apart
from the rest. SCORE builds environments by this convention, with the
creator deciding and the framework doing the departments' work. The
creator locks an environment concept (EC): its style and its physics.
The EC holds the scenes and their layering. For each scene a concept is
then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene, and from it the
framework makes every layer below, each its own OpenUSD layer, checked
before the next is made.

World models generate an environment as frames {[}4{]}, {[}5{]} that no
engine can open. 3D environment generators {[}6{]}, {[}7{]} produce
geometry, but mostly as one fused asset, from which separate objects are
recovered after the fact {[}8{]}, {[}9{]}. Both work in one shot, so a
creator cannot find or change the step behind a fault. Procedural
systems {[}10{]}, {[}11{]} and coding agents that write scenes as
programs {[}12{]}, {[}13{]}, {[}14{]}, {[}15{]}, {[}16{]}, {[}17{]} keep
the environment explicit, but rebuild a scene that already exists or
draw their objects from a fixed library; their models often float or
come apart {[}18{]}, and such agents judge their own geometry unreliably
{[}19{]}; on scenes rebuilt from video, the best model scores 0.91 on
static scenes but 0.67 on dynamic ones {[}20{]}, which is why SCORE
checks motion as a layer of its own. Open vision foundation models
{[}21{]} now read shapes {[}22{]}, masks {[}23{]}, depth {[}24{]} and
parts {[}25{]} out of pictures, and a coding agent can connect them as
tools; none of them provides one style across objects that different
models made, which the field names as an open problem {[}26{]},
{[}27{]}.

SCORE's environments are meant to be \textbf{complete}: every object,
surface, light and sound exists wherever the viewer looks. They are
\textbf{owned}: every model in the framework allows commercial use of
its output, so the creator stays the author, holding the full commercial
rights. They are meant to be \textbf{responsive}: every object has real
collision, mass and material. And they can be any kind of
\textbf{environment}, from a house on a plot of ground to a system of
worlds. To our knowledge, no other openly available environment
generation work joins vision foundation models into engine-ready
environments that are built from reference pictures, steered by a
person's choices at each step, and made only with models that allow
commercial use of their output.

We contribute \textbf{the SCORE framework}, which builds an environment
by the studios' convention, the creator deciding top down and the
framework making every layer below (Section 2); \textbf{measured gates}
for every layer of a scene and a completion gate over them (Section 3);
and \textbf{evidence from a first environment}, left as gaps until its
remake has run (Section 4).

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

The creator works in the creator cockpit, one signed-in web page: there
he decides the design, buys paid work and reviews what the framework
reports. Below him, an environment is a table of stages; environment 1's
has 149 rows. A \textbf{node} is one stage for one scene
(\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the environment, and one runner makes,
judges and accepts every node. From an approved design everything runs
without a further yes.

\subsection{Keys, gates and accepts}\label{keys-gates-and-accepts}

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record.
The key hashes the stage and scene, a code version, the contracts of its
output and inputs, its parameters, a seed derived from the
environment's, the pinned model images, every environment file it reads
and the accepted output of every node it reads. A fault fixed in one
stage moves only its key and those after it. Outputs are kept by content
hash, never overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown,
with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown
blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate
passed and it is no worse than the one accepted before: no answer
dropped, no measure moved the wrong way by a step a viewer could see, no
gate missing. Later nodes read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps
four actions, buttons in the cockpit: reject an accepted output (it
moves back to the one before, and every node that read it is stale),
accept a worse one with a reason, waive one gate for one key, and
override the runner for one paid run. A barrier keeps agents from them:
a store that refuses the creator's records from an agent's process, and
a hook.

\textbf{Batches.} Paid work is bought as a batch, with one estimate and
one ETA; the creator's yes to that estimate is the only spend decision,
and the batch stops at its high estimate plus 10 percent. Inside a batch
every paid job runs once, with no retry; a failed output becomes a batch
gap that the completion gate lists, and spending more on it is a new
batch. Each node's review page is built from the store alone, and the
creator reviews a scene in a 3D viewer on the store's copy of its
accepted assembly.

\subsection{From concept to scene}\label{from-concept-to-scene}

\textbf{The environment concept.} The creator first locks the EC, in a
short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro {[}28{]} in the cockpit: its style
and its \textbf{physics}, with his reference pictures. The EC holds the
scenes and their layering: the top scenes and the child scenes each may
hold. Its physics is what everything stands on and in and how it moves,
at the scale he sets: a plot of ground with a gravity and a sun path,
one world, or a system of bodies that move one another, a real body
taking its values from a cited table and an invented one given by its
radius, mass, orbit and spin. A body near enough to matter moves in the
physics simulation, a far source of light is a light, and what is too
far to move is a backdrop. An environment plan places each top scene's
site on that ground at true size and works out the sky each site sees.

\textbf{A concept for each scene, top down.} For each scene a concept is
then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene. Each draw is one
Nano Banana Pro {[}29{]} call that the creator's click pays for, on a
concept review page where he marks areas and asks for a new draw. Every
draw is sent a \textbf{blockout} first, a grey model built in code from
the parent's plan at real sizes, so it keeps that layout and proportions
and adds the look. A child scene is drawn only once its parent scene is
locked and planned, on a crop of the parent's concept, so it fits the
parent it stands in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} The creator's lock is the approval. Gemini
3.1 Pro lists every object of the locked concept as a row of the scene
inventory, with its kind, count, anchor and size in metres, and SAM 3
{[}23{]} boxes each row on the concept; a row without metres, or a
disagreement between the two, is a gap. Nothing is made or placed that
is not a row. One more Gemini 3.1 Pro call writes the plan in metres,
element by element: outline, walls, openings, levels, stairs, roofs,
terrain heights and each item. Free checks follow: every row placed as
often as it counts, inside the outline, no two floor items in each
other, every door and standing spot reached on foot, and the child
scenes tiling their parent. On the plan review page the creator corrects
the plan, and his edits redo what follows. Only a scene with a design
gets the rows of a batch.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route is one loop, the same at every scale. A
parent scene gets its concept, its plan and a rough model, its concept
put through picture to 3D whole, which gives the plan its proportions.
Its plan is then split into the shapes of its child scenes, and once
those shapes are fixed every child scene is made in parallel, each
repeating the loop (the creator's order of 19 October 2026). The
runner's rows \texttt{rough-model} and \texttt{shell-split} (gated on
\texttt{tiled}, \texttt{reached} and \texttt{bounded}) are built and
have not run.

\textbf{The scene record.} The scene record holds what no made piece
covers (shells, stairs, ground and water, backdrop, lights, sky, the
neighbours a scene shows), written from the plan, inventory, made ground
and sky, and gated on \texttt{plan-matched} (the shell within 5 cm of
the plan), \texttt{openings} (every doorway onto a floor, no view into
nothing) and \texttt{seed-matched} (every old entry matched or dropped
with a reason).

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every scene is one OpenUSD stage {[}3{]};
each object, one per real-world object, carries its kind, transform,
collision and each part's library surface. Each department's work is a
layer, in a fixed order: a slot for the creator's edit layer on top,
then people, sound, light, motion, effects, crops, machines, settle,
ground and the generated base. Each scene is assembled once, from every
layer's accepted output; its \texttt{layers} gate answers unknown while
a needed layer has none, and an old output never stands in. A scene
shows its neighbours by reference to their own accepted assemblies. The
edit layer is still a slot: a test on a two-box scene shows an edit
there surviving a regenerated base, but the assembly does not yet carry
the creator's edits from one assembly to the next.

\textbf{Models, generation first.} Every object goes the prop route
first: Nano Banana Pro draws its close-up once from its crop of the
concept, Pixal3D {[}22{]} builds it, the surface stage paints it, and a
likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with its close-up. A
close-up that fails the shape check is a batch gap, so Pixal3D is never
paid for it. Only an object whose checks fail is built by a code builder
a coding agent writes (the creator's choice, 12 October 2026). A big
object the viewer walks up to gets a body built in code under the
generated details its entry names (the creator, 18 October 2026).

\section{Stages and their gates}\label{stages-and-their-gates}

\subsection{Surfaces: the finish route}\label{surfaces-the-finish-route}

Everything a scene shows comes from the creator's library, score\_lib,
or is a written exception (the creator's rule of 18 October 2026).
Models give shape only: a picture-made model keeps its geometry and its
finish is replaced. Every part takes one surface from a shared library
of rule-based surfaces, written as ProcFunc functions {[}30{]} on
Infinigen's shaders {[}10{]}, coloured from the scene's palette and worn
only where wear has a cause (Appendix D).

The finish route gives each model its finishes (the creator's choice of
20 October 2026). Free renders cover every face of the raw model, and
each view is split into colour regions. One Gemini 3.1 Pro answer a
model lists its finishes, each matched first to a library surface with
its settings, and labels each region of each view; the labels vote
across views into one finish a face, and a face no label reaches or
whose views disagree is a gap. Each part is painted from its library
surface and baked, and the bake is checked against the close-up: a
finish's surface must lie within ΔE 32 of its colour there, a bake fails
when it darkens a finish the close-up shows lighter or turns all
surfaces into one grey, and a foot over 3 cm off the floor fails. A free
library check traces every material, model, light, sound and character
of a scene's accepted assembly to a library entry or a written
exception.

\subsection{Resting}\label{resting}

Loose objects are dropped onto the made ground in a physics simulation;
one that would turn more than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm is a
resting fault of the layout and keeps its laid pose. The settle stage
lays where each object comes to rest as a layer (held objects snapped
onto their holders, a stack onto one support, an object that would rest
worse kept as laid), gated on \texttt{rested} and \texttt{resting} (no
fault that appears with the layer and not without it).

\subsection{Light}\label{light}

Lamp strengths are measured. The light layer places live lamps from the
scene record, with the sky and its bodies. One paid render per scene
gives every lamp its own Cycles light group on the scene's views;
because light adds up, the look at any strengths is mixed from these
passes without rendering again, and a free sweep chooses the scale that
brings the scene's brightness within 1.5 times its concept's, halving
any lamp that turns what it lights white.

\subsection{Motion, effects and sound}\label{motion-effects-and-sound}

\textbf{Motion.} Each moving thing (a path, a hinge, a spin, a rail, a
turn, a climb) has its kind's speed range cited from real machines,
gated on \texttt{clear} (no moving thing within 3 cm of a still thing or
another moving thing, read every quarter second), \texttt{speeds},
\texttt{loops}, \texttt{grounded} and \texttt{covered}. \textbf{Effects}
are emitters measured against real footage, gated on \texttt{attached},
\texttt{footage}, \texttt{light} and \texttt{outside} (no open-sky
particle inside a room). \textbf{Sound:} every source a scene names gets
the environment's picked file or one MOSS-SoundEffect {[}31{]} take of
its brief, gated on \texttt{sources}, \texttt{level}, \texttt{likeness}
(CLAP {[}32{]} score at least 0.30; a take under it is a gap) and
\texttt{no\ freesound}.

\subsection{People}\label{people}

People are made like props: a FLUX.2 klein {[}33{]} close-up in SOMA-X's
bind pose, built by Pixal3D, painted by the surface line, over SOMA-X's
body fitted just inside, whose skin weights each look point takes from
its closest triangle; Kimodo's clips {[}34{]}, from sentences, move it.
The \textbf{shell gate} passes a look when 99 percent of its soft points
lie within 5 cm of the body, its worst point recorded; each clip gets
its own verdict (tucks, hard parts stretched, cloth or body through
cloth), and casts use only passing clips. \textbf{Casts:} each person of
a scene's inventory is a look of the people line, SAM 3 finds the
concept's figures, and casts are gated on \texttt{counted},
\texttt{standing}, \texttt{clear}, \texttt{made} and \texttt{drapes}.

\subsection{Completion}\label{completion}

A scene is complete when its gate passes on its final assembly: every
row made or dropped with a reason, nothing unmade, no placeholder or
resting fault, the creator's picks shown and every layer and shown scene
present. A fault the creator decides to ship with is an accepted gap,
written with who decided, when, why and its measured faults; the
completion gate lists every gap by name and is never loosened, and a
scene whose accepted gaps are its only fail is complete apart from
accepted gaps. A hook stops an agent claiming a scene is done while its
gate fails: agents judging their own scenes agree with a measured
geometry score only 45.8 percent of the time {[}19{]}.

\subsection{Prices and ledgers}\label{prices-and-ledgers}

Every paid stage is priced from a unit cost measured on the ledgers,
times the units its forecast rule counts, times the bill's share over
the ledger; a stage with no measured run has no price, and its estimate
is shown apart as a guess. Machines and Google calls write ledger lines
with their node's clearance, and the month's spend is the higher of
ledgers and bill, which caught misses: EUR 1,554 billed against EUR
1,387 in the cloud ledger on 12 October 2026, and EUR 549 billed by
Google for a day the earlier ledger put at EUR 30. A paid node runs only
when every gate on its inputs passed, the month's books are known and
its batch's stop has room for its forecast.

\section{Limitations, future work and
conclusion}\label{limitations-future-work-and-conclusion}

One creator judges, and he has not yet accepted a run. Closed models
(Nano Banana Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro) draw the concepts and close-ups and
read and plan every scene; they may change or go. The design step, the
finish route, the shapes-first loop and the code bodies have not yet
carried a scene through a batch; the edit layer is a slot, objects are
not interactive, and no game engine opens the result yet. Next: the
remake's batch, an engine adapter with physics and interaction, an edit
layer the assembly carries, three more environments, open models,
LEGO-Bench {[}19{]}. In short, SCORE builds an environment the way
studios do, scene by scene and layer by layer, so a fault is fixed in
its own layer; an accepted environment will show if it holds.
