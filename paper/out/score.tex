\title{SCORE: A Framework for Synthesizing Complete, Owned, Responsive
Environments}
\author{J.P. Kardolus, Babon Innovations B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands}
\date{Working draft, 12 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Games, films and robot training need 3D worlds that are complete,
editable and owned by the people who make them. World models and
one-shot 3D generators can produce a convincing place from a sentence or
a picture, but they give frames that no engine can open, or one fused
scene without separate objects or clear rights. Studios have always
built worlds in stages, from concept art and layout to modelling,
surfacing, sound and review. We present SCORE, a world generation
framework that keeps these stages and automates large parts of them with
open vision foundation models, procedural tools and coding agents, while
the creator chooses and reviews. Because every stage's output is kept,
the creator can rerun or fix any single stage, and because every model
allows commercial use, the creator stays the author of the result,
holding its full commercial rights. We are building SCORE's first world,
the game world of 2099, only 3 of whose 18 places passed its completion
gate when it was last measured, and plan three further worlds of
different kinds. Our aim is to show that checkable stages, with open
models for shape and closed, paid models for pictures and surfaces, can
build worlds that a creator owns and keeps editing. Code and paper are
available at https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT
license).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Interactive 3D worlds are the raw material of games, film and embodied
AI {[}1{]}, and building one is expensive: every object has to be
modelled, surfaced, placed, given collision, lit and given sound in one
style, and the world has to stay editable. Generative models promise to
take over much of this, but in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game
workers thought generative AI was hurting the industry {[}2{]}; a tool
for building worlds has to leave the result in the creator's hands.

World models generate a world as frames {[}3{]}, {[}4{]} that no engine
can open. 3D world generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} produce geometry, but
mostly as one fused asset, from which separate objects are recovered
after the fact {[}7{]}, {[}8{]}. Both work in one shot, so a creator
cannot find or change the step behind a fault. Procedural systems
{[}9{]}, {[}10{]} and coding agents that write scenes as programs
{[}11{]}, {[}12{]}, {[}13{]}, {[}14{]} keep the world explicit, but
rebuild a scene that already exists or draw their objects from a fixed
library, and such agents judge their own geometry unreliably {[}15{]}.
Open vision foundation models {[}16{]} now read shapes {[}17{]}, masks
{[}18{]}, depth {[}19{]} and parts {[}20{]} out of pictures, and a
coding agent can connect them as tools; none of them provides is one
style across objects that different models made, which the field names
as an open problem {[}21{]}, {[}22{]}.

Studios build worlds in stages, each with an output a person can check.
SCORE keeps these stages and automates large parts of them with the
models above, procedural tools and coding agents, while the creator
chooses and reviews. Its worlds are \textbf{complete}: every object,
surface, light and sound exists wherever the player looks. They are
\textbf{owned}: every model in the framework allows commercial use of
its output, so the creator stays the author, holding the full commercial
rights. They are \textbf{responsive}: every object has real collision,
mass and material. And they can be any kind of \textbf{environment}. To
our knowledge, no other openly available world generation work joins
vision foundation models into engine-ready scenes that are built from
reference pictures, steered by a person's choices at each step, and made
only with models that allow commercial use of their output.

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The SCORE framework:} staged, retraceable generation from a
  creator's choices to one canonical scene: a generated base layer under
  the creator's edit layers (Section 2).
\item
  \textbf{Mechanisms that keep worlds coherent and faults cheap:} checks
  before every paid stage, a parts check, a shared surface library that
  keeps shape and surface apart, and review tools (Section 3).
\item
  \textbf{Evidence from four worlds of different kinds}, and a shared
  benchmark (Section 4).
\end{itemize}

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio,alt={The stages on the hub of the game 2099: (a) the concept the creator chose, (b) the plan in metres, (c) part of the inventory, (d) two close-ups, (e) one library surface at three wear settings, (f) the room in the game engine.}]{figures/fig-overview.jpg}
\caption{The stages on the hub of the game 2099: (a) the concept the
creator chose, (b) the plan in metres, (c) part of the inventory, (d)
two close-ups, (e) one library surface at three wear settings, (f) the
room in the game engine.}
\end{figure}

Figure 1 shows the stages on one place, from the creator's concept to
the assembled room. Every stage's output is kept, so the creator can
trace a weak result to its stage and rerun or fix that stage alone.
Appendix C lists every model, its licence, and what was automatic or
done by hand.

\textbf{Inventory.} A coding agent cuts the concept into tiles and lists
every visible element, and each element becomes a row, with its kind,
anchor, size and count, or is dropped with a written reason. Nothing is
made or placed that is not a row.

\textbf{Close-ups.} Every inventory row gets a close-up, a picture of
the object alone, drawn from its crop of the concept by an open model,
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 {[}23{]}; only a close-up that fails the shape
check (Section 3.1) is redrawn by a closed one, Nano Banana Pro
{[}24{]}.

\textbf{Models.} Each object is built by a code builder that a coding
agent writes or generated from its close-up by Pixal3D {[}17{]}, as the
parts check decides (Section 3.2). Every real-world object becomes one
model: a console becomes a desk carrying separate monitors and
keyboards. Models give shape only (Section 3.3). Outdoor ground follows
a plan of flat areas and paths, with landforms from a picture drawn over
it.

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every place is one OpenUSD stage {[}25{]}
in which each object carries its kind, its transform in metres, its
collision and the library surface of each part, together with the room's
shell, the ground, the backdrop, the lights and the cameras the place is
judged from. The generated base layer lies under an edit layer that
belongs to the creator, so an edit survives when the base is generated
again (Figure 2).

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio,alt={The wreck as an OpenUSD stage: (a) in the game; (b) rendered in Blender from the same camera; (c) Blender's object outlines over the game's picture; (d) after an edit in the edit layer (cover moved, hull given another surface) and a regenerated base.}]{figures/fig-usd.jpg}
\caption{The wreck as an OpenUSD stage: (a) in the game; (b) rendered in
Blender from the same camera; (c) Blender's object outlines over the
game's picture; (d) after an edit in the edit layer (cover moved, hull
given another surface) and a regenerated base.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The other layers.} The same stage holds the rest of what makes a
place complete, each in a layer of its own: people, as skinned
characters that a chain of open models makes from a picture or a few
words; sound, generated by MOSS-SoundEffect {[}26{]} and tied to
surfaces, rooms and objects; motion, as time samples; particle effects;
and shaders, baked into pictures any USD reader can draw. Every stage
that loads a model runs as a batch on rented cards that are deleted
afterwards, or as a job on a Kubernetes cluster.

\section{Mechanisms}\label{mechanisms}

\subsection{Checks before spending}\label{checks-before-spending}

Deterministic checks run before every stage that costs money, because a
fault is free to fix in the plan and costly once built. They test real
geometry: light leaks, blocked openings, doors, open, thin or leaning
models, and colours off the palette. The close-ups' shape check measures
framing and outline, and an open vision-language model, Qwen3.8-27B
{[}27{]}, compares each picture with its crop; it leans towards failing,
because a wrong picture that passes costs a bad 3D model (Appendix C).
Loose objects are dropped in a physics simulation, and one that would
move further than a small limit fails the resting check, because a large
move is a fault of the layout. A failed check sends the work back one
stage (Appendix B).

Coding agents judging their own scenes agree with a measured score on
geometry only 45.8 percent of the time {[}15{]}, so a completion gate
passes a place only when every inventory row has its object or a written
reason, nothing visible stands in for a made piece, no resting fault is
left, every model the creator picked is still the one shown, and every
check ran on the stage as it is now; unknown blocks like fail, and a
hook refuses to end an agent's session that claims a place is done while
its gate does not pass.

\subsection{The parts check}\label{the-parts-check}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio,alt={The parts check: four close-ups (top) and the objects as built (bottom). The notice board and locker are built in code, whose builds show every part; the microscope and chair are generated with Pixal3D.}]{figures/fig-parts-check.jpg}
\caption{The parts check: four close-ups (top) and the objects as built
(bottom). The notice board and locker are built in code, whose builds
show every part; the microscope and chair are generated with Pixal3D.}
\end{figure}

Picture-to-3D models build chunky solids well, but flat panels, thin
beams and openings badly, while code builders are exact but show only
the parts the coding agent wrote. For each kind of object, the parts
check renders a code build from the front: if every part of the close-up
is visible in it, the kind is built in code, and otherwise it is
generated and made solid (Figure 3).

\subsection{Shape and surface apart}\label{shape-and-surface-apart}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio,alt={(a) Three library surfaces at wear 0, 0.4 and 0.8. (b) The hub's tool board with each piece's own picture colours (left) and with library surfaces, one model per tool (right).}]{figures/fig-surfaces.jpg}
\caption{(a) Three library surfaces at wear 0, 0.4 and 0.8. (b) The
hub's tool board with each piece's own picture colours (left) and with
library surfaces, one model per tool (right).}
\end{figure}

Every part of every object takes one surface from a shared library of
rule-based surfaces, written as ProcFunc functions {[}28{]} on
Infinigen's shaders {[}9{]}, coloured from the place's palette and worn
only where wear has a cause, such as edges (Figure 4a, Appendix D).
PartCrafter {[}20{]} splits a generated model into parts, and an open
vision-language model, shown each part outlined on the close-up, names
its library surface; the picture's colours are not kept. When each piece
kept them, every piece brought its own rust and stains (Figure 4b); with
shape and surface apart, the hub became consistent and the creator
accepted it (Appendix A).

\begin{center}\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.94\linewidth}\setlength{\parskip}{4pt}\small

\textbf{Gap: painting beyond the hub.} On the rest of world 1 the
painting is not yet consistent (Limitations). The surface stage's new
method, to be measured there, is the finish-map route: Gemini 3.1 Pro
{[}29{]} lists a model's finishes on its close-up and names a library
surface for each, Nano Banana Pro draws the close-up again with one flat
colour per finish, and SegviGen {[}30{]} lays that map onto the shape.

\end{minipage}}\end{center}

\subsection{Review tools}\label{review-tools}

A review page, built only from the files the stages wrote, shows each
stage's output, before-and-after shots from the same cameras, every
check's result, and the stage rendered by Blender along a walk through
it. An optional world step can turn the concept into a walkable room to
show what the concept does not; nothing it produces is shipped.

left fewer faults. Each arm ran once (Appendix E).

\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{One creator, one world so far, and that world not yet
  accepted.} Every verdict is one creator's. When last measured, only 3
  of world 1's 18 places passed their own completion gate, and the
  creator has accepted one place, the hub.
\item
  \textbf{Code builders do not scale, and places come out sparser than
  their concepts.}
\item
  \textbf{Two stages depend on closed, paid models.} Nano Banana Pro
  draws the concepts and every close-up that the open model's pictures
  fail; on the last 17 close-ups of world 1, none of the open model's
  pictures passed the shape check (Appendix C). The surface stage uses
  two of Google's models: Gemini 3.1 Pro lists each model's finishes on
  its close-up and names their library surfaces, and Nano Banana Pro
  draws the finish map that SegviGen lays onto the shape. A public
  framework thus depends on closed services for two of its stages. Their
  daily limits set the pace of world 1, and they can change their price
  or be retired, which limits reproducibility. An open-model fallback
  for both stages is future work.
\item
  \textbf{Parts do not follow finishes, and shadow reads as paint.} A
  finish without its own part, such as a gold foil patch on a white
  hull, is lost when the part is painted with one surface, and the judge
  reads shadow on the close-up as dark paint.
\item
  \textbf{Objects are physical but not interactive.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}\label{future-work}

Building the three further worlds; a game engine adapter that writes
mass and friction; interactable objects, which robotics needs; a surface
stage that holds on world 1; open close-ups that pass the shape check
more often; and LEGO-Bench {[}15{]}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

SCORE builds worlds from a creator's choices in checkable stages whose
outputs are kept. On its first world, the parts check, one model per
real-world object and the shared surface library made one room, the hub,
consistent enough for its creator to accept it. Only 3 of that world's
18 places passed the completion gate when last measured; whether the
framework holds across a whole world, and across kinds of worlds, is
what the remaining places, worlds and the benchmark will show.

Three findings shaped the method. First, deterministic checks catch what
agents miss: in round one, the share of rays leaking out through walls
and roof fell from 1.53 percent of 5,817 to zero, and the colour noise
within surfaces from a median of 31.1 to 3.2 percent, but only walking
through the loaded room showed 26 old models still in it, which became
the made-only check. Second, generated pieces that kept their pictures'
colours made the room inconsistent, so surfaces come only from the
library. Third, generated composite objects melted their parts together,
and generated boxes leaned (one locker by 13 degrees), which led to one
model per real-world object and to the straightness check (Figure 4b).
By round six the room used 0.45 million triangles, against 1.96 million
in round four, and 187 MB on disk, against 487 MB.

\section{Check details}\label{check-details}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Room checks} cast rays to find light leaking out through walls
  and roof, find pieces in front of a real opening and roof and wall
  fittings more than 3 cm off their surface or turned more than 5
  degrees from it, and count pieces, triangles and lamps against the
  room's budget.
\item
  \textbf{Door check:} every door must stand in a wall or partition that
  fully separates its two sides, tested by walking a grid from one side
  to the other.
\item
  \textbf{Parts check:} in the code build, every part shown in the
  close-up must be at least 10 percent visible from the front, and no
  label may cover a part.
\item
  \textbf{Model check:} every model must have walls at least 3 mm thick;
  whether a model is closed is recorded on each model and reported, not
  used to refuse it. A generated box fails if one of its sides tilts
  more than 2 degrees or is warped. Making a generated model solid stops
  if its bounding box grows more than 3 percent.
\item
  \textbf{Palette sweep:} every model's colours are compared with the
  place's library palette.
\item
  \textbf{Made-only check:} in the engine, any visible mesh that the
  framework did not make fails. A made-only finding can never be waived.
\item
  \textbf{Close-up shape check:} the picture needs a plain border all
  round, one object filling a sensible share of the frame, and an
  outline within a set proportion of the inventory row's box. The
  judge's seven questions (among them one object, a clean background,
  the whole object, the same object, no parts missing or added, the
  right proportions) each pass by a majority of three answers sampled
  with different seeds. A close-up passes only when both halves pass.
\item
  \textbf{Settling and resting check:} an object that would turn more
  than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm when dropped keeps its laid
  pose and is marked as not resting; small debris, with no side over 1.4
  m, may turn freely and drift up to 1 m. Hung objects are exempt by
  their inventory anchor, and fixed objects are not judged for tipping.
\item
  \textbf{People checks:} a person's look rides SOMA-X's body fitted
  just inside it. The shell is a proxy gate: it passes when 99\% of a
  look's soft points lie within 5 cm of the fitted body, with the hard
  parts (boots, helmets, tools), the hair and the hands riding one joint
  each and belts worn as cloth, and the per-clip checks (body through
  cloth, cloth through cloth, tucks) decide which clips a look may use.
  The A-pose outline is judged against the finished Pixal3D take the
  build starts from; the take's own loss against the raw model is
  recorded on its own.
\item
  \textbf{Completion gate:} the requirements given in Section 3.1, each
  with its result tied to a fingerprint of the stage's files and inputs;
  a result made on another stage counts as unknown. The placeholder
  check judges every mesh a code builder draws, and only the shell
  builders pass unnamed.
\end{itemize}

The picture-to-3D step, the part splitting, the surface bakes, the sound

\section{The surface library}\label{the-surface-library}

The surface library holds families of variants: painted, steel,
aluminium, rubber, plastic, cable, fabric, composite, glass, screen,
light and print. After the hub's second round it held 71 variants made
from 14 ProcFunc recipes. Each variant is a recipe with settings, takes
its colours only from the palette, and is baked into base-colour,
metal-roughness and normal maps at a texture density that depends on how
close the player can get. Wear has three levels and comes from causes:
edges, and kicks within 32 cm of the floor that a piece stands on.

\section{With and without the agent
tools}\label{with-and-without-the-agent-tools}

The tools that check an agent's work were tested on three places of
world 1: a room (the workshop, 814 objects), an outdoor place (the
street, 1,198 objects) and a dense room (the hangar, 1,657 objects).
They are the completion gate (Section 3.1); a triage of the resting
check, which judges each object by what holds it up, read from its
inventory row, and keeps only real faults {[}51{]}, {[}52{]}; a pick
lock, which refuses an export that would show anything but the model the
creator picked for a row; and a render-and-compare, which lists the
objects least like their close-ups by DINOv2 similarity {[}16{]}. In
both arms, the same agent (Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code, headless)
started from the same exported stage and inventory and was asked to make
the place as complete as it could in about 50 minutes, through its
layout data only; one arm had the framework from before these tools.
Each arm ran once, so Table E1 shows what happened, not an average.

The tools made building cheaper more often than they made the scene
better. On the workshop and the street, the arm with the tools used
about half the agent's output and cost, and exported the stage once or
twice instead of seven to nine times, because the triage named the few
real faults among hundreds of alarms. On the workshop, 13 of its 18
faults were cleared by writing into the inventory what holds a piece up,
which the creator still has to confirm. On the hangar the arm without
the tools left fewer faults: the arm with them spent its time replacing
five placeholder slabs and trying the drop test, which failed on a room
with no ground mesh (fixed after the run), and the nine faults it left
are models with stray parts below their feet. No agent claimed a place
was complete, so the gate's hook never had to refuse a claim. No row
carries an owner's pick, so the pick lock was not tested.
