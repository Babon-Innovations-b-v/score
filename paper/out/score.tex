\title{SCORE: A Framework for Synthesizing Complete, Owned, Responsive
Environments}
\author{J.P. Kardolus, Babon Innovations B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands}
\date{Working draft, 13 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Games, films and robot training need 3D worlds that are complete,
editable and owned by the people who make them. World models and
one-shot 3D generators can produce a convincing place from a sentence or
a picture, but they give frames that no engine can open, or one fused
scene without separate objects or clear rights. Studios have always
built worlds in stages, from concept art and layout to modelling,
surfacing, lighting, sound and review. We present SCORE, a world
generation framework that keeps these stages and automates them with
open vision foundation models, procedural tools and coding agents, while
the creator chooses and reviews. Every stage is a node of one runner:
its output is keyed on its inputs and its code, judged by gates that
answer pass, fail or unknown, and accepted only when nothing got worse,
so a fault is fixed in its own stage and only what follows it runs
again. Paid steps are cleared against a month's budget read from the
provider's own bill. Every model in the framework allows commercial use
of its output, so the creator stays the author of the result. We
describe the framework as built for its first world, the game world of
2099 with 18 places, and the measured gates of each of its layers:
surfaces, resting, light, motion, effects, sound and people. Results per
place are being gathered. Code and paper are available at
https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT license).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Interactive 3D worlds are the raw material of games, film and embodied
AI {[}1{]}, and building one is expensive: every object has to be
modelled, surfaced, placed, given collision, lit and given sound in one
style, and the world has to stay editable. Generative models promise to
take over much of this, but in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game
workers thought generative AI was hurting the industry {[}2{]}; a tool
for building worlds has to leave the result in the creator's hands.

World models generate a world as frames {[}3{]}, {[}4{]} that no engine
can open. 3D world generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} produce geometry, but
mostly as one fused asset, from which separate objects are recovered
after the fact {[}7{]}, {[}8{]}. Both work in one shot, so a creator
cannot find or change the step behind a fault. Procedural systems
{[}9{]}, {[}10{]} and coding agents that write scenes as programs
{[}11{]}, {[}12{]}, {[}13{]}, {[}14{]} keep the world explicit, but
rebuild a scene that already exists or draw their objects from a fixed
library, and such agents judge their own geometry unreliably {[}15{]}.
Open vision foundation models {[}16{]} now read shapes {[}17{]}, masks
{[}18{]}, depth {[}19{]} and parts {[}20{]} out of pictures, and a
coding agent can connect them as tools; none of them provides one style
across objects that different models made, which the field names as an
open problem {[}21{]}, {[}22{]}.

Studios build worlds in stages, each with an output a person can check.
SCORE keeps these stages and automates them with the models above,
procedural tools and coding agents, while the creator chooses and
reviews. Its worlds are \textbf{complete}: every object, surface, light
and sound exists wherever the player looks. They are \textbf{owned}:
every model in the framework allows commercial use of its output, so the
creator stays the author, holding the full commercial rights. They are
\textbf{responsive}: every object has real collision, mass and material.
And they can be any kind of \textbf{environment}. To our knowledge, no
other openly available world generation work joins vision foundation
models into engine-ready scenes that are built from reference pictures,
steered by a person's choices at each step, and made only with models
that allow commercial use of their output.

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The SCORE framework:} stages as nodes of one runner that keys,
  judges and accepts every output, over one canonical scene: a generated
  base under the creator's edit layer (Section 2).
\item
  \textbf{Stages with measured gates} for every layer of a place:
  models, surfaces, resting, light, motion, effects, sound and people,
  and a completion gate over all of them (Section 3).
\item
  \textbf{Evidence from a first world}, with its spend per stage read
  from the ledgers and the bill (Section 4).
\end{itemize}

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

A world is a table of stages; world 1's has 95 rows. A \textbf{node} is
one stage for one place (\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the whole
world, and one runner makes, judges and accepts every node. The
creator's picks (the concept of each place, the style, the references)
are the inputs; from there everything runs without a typed yes, and the
creator reviews.

\subsection{Keys, gates and accepts}\label{keys-gates-and-accepts}

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record.
The key hashes the stage and place, a code version, the contracts of its
output and inputs, its parameters, a seed derived from the world's, the
pinned model images, every world file it reads and the accepted output
of every node it reads. The code version hashes every source file the
stage's make and gates import, walked through their imports; the runner
and the renting code are left out, since they change what a run costs,
not what it makes. A fault fixed in one stage moves the keys of that
stage and of what follows it, and nothing else. Outputs are kept by
content hash, never overwritten, each stamped with its schema in metres,
seconds and degrees.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown,
with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown
blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate
passed and it is no worse than the output accepted before: no answer
dropped, no measure moved the wrong way, no gate went missing. Later
nodes read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps four commands, from
his own terminal: reject an accepted output (it moves back to the one
before, and every node that read it is stale), accept a worse one with
his reason, waive one gate for one key, and override the runner for one
paid run. None is needed for a run. Agents are kept from them by a
barrier, not a lock: the commands want a terminal, the store refuses the
creator's records from any process an agent started, and a hook blocks
the other routes.

\textbf{Spending and review.} A paid node is cleared only when every
gate on its inputs passed and the month's ceiling has room for its
estimate (Section 3.7); every place in the code that rents a machine or
calls a paid model refuses without that ticket, and a run that would buy
again more than EUR 5 of accepted outputs needs a written reason. Each
node's review page is built from the store alone (its gates' answers and
measures, who accepted it, the command that rejects it), and the creator
reviews a place in a USD viewer on the store's copy of its accepted
assembly; nothing is rendered for review.

\subsection{From concept to scene}\label{from-concept-to-scene}

\textbf{Inventory, plan and record.} A coding agent lists every visible
element of the concept as an inventory row (kind, anchor, size, count)
or drops it with a written reason; nothing is made or placed that is not
a row. It draws a dimensioned plan, gated on \texttt{mapped} (every
element of the concept a row, a door or a written drop; every door says
where it leads). The scene record is written from the plan, inventory,
made ground and sky, gated on \texttt{game-free} (nothing copied from
the game the world came from), \texttt{plan-matched} (the shell within 5
cm of the plan), \texttt{openings} (every doorway onto a floor, no view
into nothing) and \texttt{seed-matched} (every old entry matched or
dropped with a reason).

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every place is one OpenUSD stage {[}23{]}
in which each object carries its kind, transform, collision and the
library surface of each part. Its layers lie in a fixed order, the
creator's edit layer on top, then people, sound, light, motion, effects,
crops, machines, settle, ground and the generated base, so an edit
survives a regenerated base. Each place is assembled once, from every
layer's accepted output; its \texttt{layers} gate answers unknown while
a layer the place needs has none, and an old output never stands in.

\textbf{Models, generation first.} Every object goes the prop route
first: Nano Banana Pro {[}24{]} draws its close-up from its crop of the
concept, Pixal3D {[}17{]} builds it, the surface stage paints it, and a
free likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with its
close-up. Only an object whose measured checks fail is built by a code
builder that a coding agent writes. This reverses the earlier parts
check, which built a kind in code whenever its code build showed every
part of the close-up (the creator's choice, 12 October 2026). Every
real-world object stays one model: a console is a desk carrying separate
monitors. Most of world 1's models predate the change and enter as
unverified seeds, judged by the stages after them.

\section{Stages and their gates}\label{stages-and-their-gates}

\subsection{Surfaces: the finish map}\label{surfaces-the-finish-map}

Models give shape only; every part takes one surface from a shared
library of rule-based surfaces, written as ProcFunc functions {[}25{]}
on Infinigen's shaders {[}9{]}, coloured from the place's palette and
worn only where wear has a cause (Appendix D). Gemini 3.1 Pro {[}26{]}
lists the close-up's finishes and names a library surface for each. Nano
Banana Pro draws the close-up again with one flat colour per finish, up
to three times until the map fits the close-up's outline, then draws
unlit renders of the raw model's sides and back against that map.
SegviGen {[}27{]} splits the mesh guided by the close-up's map, every
map votes on the faces its view sees, and a part whose second finish
holds a fifth of it fails. Each part is painted from its library surface
and baked; the picture's own colours are never kept, because when every
piece kept them, every piece brought its own rust and stains (Appendix
A).

The bake is checked against the close-up. A finish's surface must lie
within ΔE 32 of its colour there, else a nearer surface of its family is
taken or a new one is proposed to the creator, and the part fails by
name. Two checks came from the greenhouse's water tank, which the
creator found baked as three near greys and standing tipped: a bake
fails when it turns a finish the close-up shows lighter into a darker
one, or all its surfaces into one grey the close-up does not have; and a
model on legs has its short legs stretched onto one level plane before
its bake, a foot more than 3 cm off the floor failing. Each model is
dirtied as much as its close-up shows, capped at its place's dirt.

\subsection{Resting}\label{resting}

Loose objects are dropped onto the made ground in a physics simulation;
one that would turn more than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm is a
resting fault of the layout and keeps its laid pose. The settle stage
lays where each object comes to rest as a layer of its own (held objects
snapped onto their holders, a stack onto one support, an object that
would rest worse kept as laid). Its gates are \texttt{rested} and
\texttt{resting} (no fault that appears with the layer and not without
it).

\subsection{Light}\label{light}

Lamp strengths are measured, never copied from the source game. The
light layer places live lamps where the scene record puts them, with the
sky, the sun and, seen from the Moon, the Earth. One paid render per
place gives every lamp its own Cycles light group on the place's views;
because light adds up, the look at any strengths is mixed from these
passes without rendering again, and a free sweep chooses the scale that
brings the place's brightness within 1.5 times its concept's, halving
any lamp that turns what it lights white. Under the open sky the suns
are swept after the lamps. A lamp is measured where it shines: one whose
fixture stops 90 percent of its rays moves out of the fixture, and one
behind a clear lens stands just outside it, since Cycles passes no light
through a lens that also glows. Baking a pass per lamp, mixed live in
the engine, is planned.

\subsection{Motion, effects and sound}\label{motion-effects-and-sound}

\textbf{Motion.} Each moving thing is a mover in the place's record (a
path, a hinge, a spin, a rail, a turn, a climb) with its kind's speed
range cited from real machines. The gates read the composed stage every
quarter second: \texttt{clear} (no mover within 3 cm of a still thing or
another mover), \texttt{speeds}, \texttt{loops}, \texttt{grounded} and
\texttt{covered} (every prim of a moving kind that stays still says
why).

\textbf{Effects} are emitters on a prim, measured against real footage:
the launch's cloud and flame in rocket heights from photographs of real
lift-offs, the camp's dust devils from rover films of Mars. The gates
are \texttt{attached}, \texttt{footage} (every measure of the baked
particles within the range read off footage), \texttt{light} (the flame
lights the pad at least twice as brightly as the place's lamps) and
\texttt{outside} (no open-sky particle inside a room).

\textbf{Sound.} Every source a place names (room tones, objects,
motions, each surface's steps and impacts) gets the world's picked file
or the best of eight MOSS-SoundEffect {[}28{]} takes of its brief, gated
on \texttt{sources}, \texttt{level} (loudness, peak and length),
\texttt{likeness} (CLAP {[}29{]} score at least 0.30, the tenth
percentile of the world's picks) and \texttt{no\ freesound}.

\subsection{People}\label{people}

People are made like props. A close-up of the dressed person in SOMA-X's
bind pose, drawn by FLUX.2 klein {[}30{]}, is built by Pixal3D and
painted by the surface line. SOMA-X's body is fitted just inside the
look (Gauss-Newton on 24 shape numbers), and every point of the look
takes the skin weights of its closest body triangle, so Kimodo's clips
{[}31{]}, written from sentences, move it. The \textbf{shell gate}
passes a look when 99 percent of its soft points lie within 5 cm of the
body, its worst point recorded. Hard parts ride one joint each (hair,
boots, bare hands, helmets), and belts are worn as cloth. Each clip gets
its own verdict (tucks, hard parts stretched, cloth through cloth, body
through cloth), and a cast uses only the clips that pass. A look seen
only far away is judged on its far body, and a look's colour comes from
the cast record's words (Appendix B).

\textbf{Casts.} SAM 3 {[}18{]} finds the figures on a place's views, and
a vision-language judge tells a person from a picture, a reflection or
an empty suit. A cast is gated on \texttt{counted}, \texttt{standing},
\texttt{clear}, \texttt{made} and \texttt{drapes}; a crowd stands only
where no copy fills a tenth of a view's height, and every person wears
their look's accepted body, never a stand-in.

\subsection{Completion}\label{completion}

A place is complete when its completion gate passes on its final
assembly: every inventory row has its object or a written reason;
nothing visible is unmade or a code placeholder; no resting fault; every
pick of the creator is the model shown; and the place holds what it
needs: its cast in made looks, its effects and motions, its crops, every
generated model held to its close-up and repainted, every lamp and room
tone of its record, every place it shows, and nothing copied from the
source game. Each answer is tied to a fingerprint of the stage; an
answer made on another stage is unknown, and a hook refuses to end an
agent's session that claims a place is done while its gate fails. Coding
agents judging their own scenes agree with a measured score on geometry
only 45.8 percent of the time {[}15{]}, so no agent's word completes a
place.

\textbf{Gap: Figure 10, responsive.} A room in a physics engine, a
dropped crate and a door reacting. The physics stage writes UsdPhysics
collision, mass and friction and runs a Bullet trial, so far on the
habitat only; until it passes, responsiveness is that trial.

\end{minipage}}\end{center}

\subsection{Budget and ledgers}\label{budget-and-ledgers}

Every rented machine writes a ledger line with its euros, and every call
to Google's models writes a started line before the call and a priced
line after it, so a call that never returns still counts. Each line
carries the ticket of its node. The month's spend is the higher of the
ledgers and the provider's bill, because the ledgers missed costs: on 12
October 2026 the month's cloud bill stood at EUR 1,554 against EUR 1,387
in the ledger (disks and addresses billed by the hour, storage, some
card time), and on 11 October Google billed EUR 549 for a day in which
the earlier ledger held EUR 30. Over 13.8 hours of world 1's run from
the first ticketed line, the ledgers hold EUR 117.45 of rented machines
and EUR 34.19 of Google's models; the largest stages were painting (EUR
37.61), the finish maps (EUR 26.59), the people's bodies (EUR 16.09) and
the light passes (EUR 10.17).

\section{Evidence}\label{evidence}

We build four worlds with the same stages: the game world of 2099 (a
prologue on Earth, a Moon base and a camp on Mars), a stylised
underwater world, a simulation world for robots, and a third-person
fantasy world. World 1 has 18 places, each its own line of the table,
assembled and judged complete once, when every layer it needs stands
accepted.

\textbf{With and without the agent tools.} On two of three places of
world 1, an agent with the completion gate and its tools used about half
the output and cost of one without them; on the third, the one without
left fewer faults. Each arm ran once (Appendix E).

\section{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Every verdict is one creator's, and he accepted one place, the hub,
before the runner existed. Closed, paid models draw the concepts, the
prop route's close-ups and the finish maps (Nano Banana Pro) and list
the finishes (Gemini 3.1 Pro); their limits set the pace, and they can
change price or be retired, which limits reproducibility. People are not
finished: belts worn as hard parts could not follow a deep forward bend.
Light is live, not baked, and was measured on earlier layouts; objects
are physical but not interactive.

\section{Future work}\label{future-work}

The three further worlds; an engine adapter with mass and friction;
interactable objects, which robotics needs; baked per-lamp light; open
models for close-ups and finish maps; a shared library of locked,
versioned parts that worlds reference instead of buying again;
LEGO-Bench {[}15{]}; and a measure of whether a place keeps one style.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

SCORE builds worlds from a creator's choices in stages whose outputs are
kept, keyed and judged, so a fault is fixed where it happens and only
what follows it runs again. On its first world, every layer of a place,
from surfaces to light and people, has gates measured against the
concept or real footage. Whether the framework holds across a whole
world, and across kinds of worlds, is what world 1's completed places
and the further worlds will show.

Three findings shaped the method. First, deterministic checks catch what
agents miss: in round one, the share of rays leaking out through walls
and roof fell from 1.53 percent of 5,817 to zero, and the colour noise
within surfaces from a median of 31.1 to 3.2 percent, but only walking
through the loaded room showed 26 old models still in it, which became
the made-only check. Second, generated pieces that kept their pictures'
colours made the room inconsistent, so surfaces come only from the
library. Third, generated composite objects melted their parts together,
and generated boxes leaned (one locker by 13 degrees), which led to one
model per real-world object and to the straightness check. By round six
the room used 0.45 million triangles, against 1.96 million in round
four, and 187 MB on disk, against 487 MB.

\section{Check details}\label{check-details}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Room checks} cast rays to find light leaking out through walls
  and roof, find pieces in front of a real opening and roof and wall
  fittings more than 3 cm off their surface or turned more than 5
  degrees from it, and count pieces, triangles and lamps against the
  room's budget.
\item
  \textbf{Door check:} every door must stand in a wall or partition that
  fully separates its two sides, tested by walking a grid from one side
  to the other.
\item
  \textbf{Model check:} every model must have walls at least 3 mm thick.
  A generated box fails if one of its sides tilts more than 2 degrees or
  is warped. Making a generated model solid stops if its bounding box
  grows more than 3 percent.
\item
  \textbf{Likeness gate:} a made model's outline from eight sides must
  overlap its close-up's by at least 0.50, a least set on the hub's
  twelve accepted generated models with a margin of 0.05, and its DINOv2
  {[}16{]} similarity must reach a least set the same way; without that
  calibration the gate answers unknown.
\item
  \textbf{Finish maps:} a close-up's map passes when its outline
  overlaps the close-up's by at least 0.85 and it uses every listed
  finish; a part whose second finish holds 20 percent of its voted area
  mixes finishes and fails.
\item
  \textbf{Bake check:} each baked part within ΔE 20 (CIE76) of its
  surface's colour and within ΔE 32 of its close-up's; lightness order
  fails when the close-up shows one surface more than 10 L* lighter and
  the bake makes it more than 3 L* darker; one look fails when every two
  surfaces bake within ΔE 12, near neutral, while the close-up shows
  them more than ΔE 24 apart; a quadrant of a standing model's base more
  than 3 cm off the floor fails. Glass, screens, lamps and print are
  judged by their own colour and left out of the order.
\item
  \textbf{Settling and resting check:} an object that would turn more
  than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm when dropped keeps its laid
  pose and is marked as not resting; small debris, with no side over 1.4
  m, may turn freely and drift up to 1 m. Hung objects are exempt by
  their inventory anchor, and fixed objects are not judged for tipping.
  The triage fails a foot off its support and an up axis leaning more
  than 3 degrees.
\item
  \textbf{Light:} the place's median brightness within 1.5 times its
  concept's and every view within 2.5 times; a lamp is blown when over
  20 percent of the pixels where it gives at least half the light are
  white, and unlit when it gives less than 0.001 percent of every view's
  light.
\item
  \textbf{Motion:} no mover within 3 cm of a still thing or another
  mover, read every 0.25 s; rounds join within 1 mm; speeds within each
  kind's cited range.
\item
  \textbf{Sound:} CLAP score against the brief at least 0.30, the tenth
  percentile of 55 of the world's picks; a take no more than 0.5 s
  longer than its brief.
\item
  \textbf{People:} the shell gate passes a look when 99 percent of its
  soft points lie within 5 cm of the fitted body; the worst point and
  its joint are recorded. A clip is usable when no frame tucks more than
  5 mm, no hard part stretches past 1.2 times its bind length, cloth
  crosses cloth no more than in the earlier body's same clip, and no
  part's edges pass through cloth deeper than 3 mm more often than at
  the bind. A far-only look is judged on its joins, likeness and the far
  body's triangles (at most 1,220); the near outfit's triangles and
  shell offset are recorded, not gating. The A-pose outline is judged
  against the finished Pixal3D take the build starts from; the take's
  own loss against the raw model is recorded apart. A crowd copy stands
  only where it fills under a tenth of every view's height.
\item
  \textbf{Completion gate:} the requirements given in Section 3.6, each
  with its result tied to a fingerprint of the stage's files and inputs;
  a result made on another stage counts as unknown. The placeholder
  check judges every mesh a code builder draws, and only the shell
  builders pass unnamed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Decisions recorded in these checks.} Three people's gates are
decisions of the captain, the agent session that runs world 1, taken on
12 and 13 October 2026 under the creator's rules and open to his
reversal. (1) The shell gate counts 99 percent of the soft points rather
than every point, with the worst point recorded: the points beyond it
were loose cloth edges (cuffs, hems, caps), which no part label names,
so they could not be given a joint of their own by label. (2) A look
seen only far away is judged on what is drawn far away, not on the near
outfit it was made from. (3) The A-pose outline is judged against the
finished take, not the raw model, so the take's own loss is not charged
to the build. Earlier, the full-body cut (which looks get bodies in full
and which only far ones) was set by the people's share of the budget.

\section{The surface library}\label{the-surface-library}

The surface library holds families of variants, among them painted,
steel, aluminium, rubber, plastic, cable, fabric, composite, glass,
screen, light, print, wood, ground, foil, water, skin and hair. After
the hub's second round it held 71 variants made from 14 ProcFunc
recipes; it now holds 369 variants in 26 families made from 22 recipes.
Each variant is a recipe with settings, takes its colours only from the
palette, and is baked into base-colour, metal-roughness and normal maps
at a texture density that depends on how close the player can get. Wear
has three levels and comes from causes: edges, and kicks within 32 cm of
the floor that a piece stands on. A surface the finish-map route
proposes enters an append-only registry and joins the library only when
the creator accepts it.

\section{With and without the agent
tools}\label{with-and-without-the-agent-tools}

The tools that check an agent's work were tested on three places of
world 1: a room (the workshop, 814 objects), an outdoor place (the
street, 1,198 objects) and a dense room (the hangar, 1,657 objects).
They are the completion gate (Section 3.6); a triage of the resting
check, which judges each object by what holds it up, read from its
inventory row, and keeps only real faults {[}52{]}, {[}53{]}; a pick
lock, which refuses an export that would show anything but the model the
creator picked for a row; and a render-and-compare, which lists the
objects least like their close-ups by DINOv2 similarity {[}16{]}. In
both arms, the same agent (Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code, headless)
started from the same exported stage and inventory and was asked to make
the place as complete as it could in about 50 minutes, through its
layout data only; one arm had the framework from before these tools.
Each arm ran once, so Table E1 shows what happened, not an average.

The tools made building cheaper more often than they made the scene
better. On the workshop and the street, the arm with the tools used
about half the agent's output and cost, and exported the stage once or
twice instead of seven to nine times, because the triage named the few
real faults among hundreds of alarms. On the workshop, 13 of its 18
faults were cleared by writing into the inventory what holds a piece up,
which the creator still has to confirm. On the hangar the arm without
the tools left fewer faults: the arm with them spent its time replacing
five placeholder slabs and trying the drop test, which failed on a room
with no ground mesh (fixed after the run), and the nine faults it left
are models with stray parts below their feet. No agent claimed a place
was complete, so the gate's hook never had to refuse a claim. No row
carries an owner's pick, so the pick lock was not tested.
