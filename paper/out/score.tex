\title{SCORE: A Framework for Synthesizing Complete, Owned, Responsive
Environments}
\author{J.P. Kardolus, Babon Innovations B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands}
\date{Working draft, 15 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Games, films and robot training need 3D worlds that are complete,
editable and owned by their makers; world models and one-shot generators
give frames no engine opens, or one fused scene without separate objects
or clear rights. Studios build worlds in stages, from concept art to
review. SCORE keeps these stages and automates them with open vision
foundation models, procedural tools and coding agents, while the creator
chooses and reviews. Every stage is a node of one runner: its output is
keyed on its inputs and code, judged by gates that answer pass, fail or
unknown, and accepted only when nothing got worse, so a fault is fixed
in its own stage and only what follows it runs again; paid steps are
cleared against a budget read from the provider's bill. Every model
allows commercial use of its output, so the creator stays the author. We
describe the framework as built for its first world, the game world of
2099 with 28 places, and the measured gates of its surfaces, resting,
light, motion, effects, sound and people. Code and paper are available
at https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT license).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Interactive 3D worlds are the raw material of games, film and embodied
AI {[}1{]}, and building one is expensive: every object is modelled,
surfaced, placed, given collision, lit and given sound in one style, and
the world stays editable. Generative models promise to take over much of
this, but in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game workers thought
generative AI was hurting the industry {[}2{]}; a tool for building
worlds has to leave the result in the creator's hands.

World models generate a world as frames {[}3{]}, {[}4{]} that no engine
can open. 3D world generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} produce geometry, but
mostly as one fused asset, from which separate objects are recovered
after the fact {[}7{]}, {[}8{]}. Both work in one shot, so a creator
cannot find or change the step behind a fault. Procedural systems
{[}9{]}, {[}10{]} and coding agents that write scenes as programs
{[}11{]}, {[}12{]}, {[}13{]}, {[}14{]} keep the world explicit, but
rebuild a scene that already exists or draw their objects from a fixed
library, and such agents judge their own geometry unreliably {[}15{]}.
Open vision foundation models {[}16{]} now read shapes {[}17{]}, masks
{[}18{]}, depth {[}19{]} and parts {[}20{]} out of pictures, and a
coding agent can connect them as tools; none of them provides one style
across objects that different models made, which the field names as an
open problem {[}21{]}, {[}22{]}.

Studios build worlds in stages, each with an output a person can check;
SCORE keeps these stages and automates them. Its worlds are
\textbf{complete}: every object, surface, light and sound exists
wherever the player looks. They are \textbf{owned}: every model in the
framework allows commercial use of its output, so the creator stays the
author, holding the full commercial rights. They are
\textbf{responsive}: every object has real collision, mass and material.
And they can be any kind of \textbf{environment}. To our knowledge, no
other openly available world generation work joins vision foundation
models into engine-ready scenes that are built from reference pictures,
steered by a person's choices at each step, and made only with models
that allow commercial use of their output.

We contribute \textbf{the SCORE framework}, stages as nodes of one
runner that keys, judges and accepts every output over one canonical
scene (Section 2); \textbf{measured gates} for every layer of a place
and a completion gate over them (Section 3); and \textbf{evidence from a
first world}, with its spend per stage (Section 4).

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

A world is a table of stages; world 1's has 95 rows. A \textbf{node} is
one stage for one place (\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the world, and
one runner makes, judges and accepts every node. The creator's picks
(concepts, style, references) are the inputs; from there everything runs
without a typed yes, and the creator reviews.

\subsection{Keys, gates and accepts}\label{keys-gates-and-accepts}

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record.
The key hashes the stage and place, a code version, the contracts of its
output and inputs, its parameters, a seed derived from the world's, the
pinned model images, every world file it reads and the accepted output
of every node it reads. The code version hashes every source file the
stage imports, leaving out the runner and the renting code, which change
what a run costs, not what it makes. A fault fixed in one stage moves
only its key and those after it. Outputs are kept by content hash, never
overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown,
with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown
blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate
passed and it is no worse than the one accepted before: no answer
dropped, no measure moved the wrong way by a step a viewer could see, no
gate missing. Later nodes read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps
four commands, from the creator's own terminal: reject an accepted
output (it moves back to the one before, and every node that read it is
stale), accept a worse one with a reason, waive one gate for one key,
and override the runner for one paid run. When the creator accepts a
worse output, that accept records what was allowed, and a later output
no worse than the one taken is accepted with that accept named. None is
needed for a run; a barrier keeps agents from them (a terminal, a store
that refuses the creator's records from an agent's process, and a hook).

\textbf{Spending and review.} A paid node is cleared only when every
gate on its inputs passed and the month's ceiling has room for its
estimate (Section 3.7); everything that rents a machine or calls a paid
model refuses without that ticket, and buying again more than EUR 5 of
accepted outputs needs a written reason. Each node's review page is
built from the store alone, and the creator reviews a place in a USD
viewer on the store's copy of its accepted assembly; nothing is rendered
for review.

\textbf{The creator steers: the ship.} Figure 1 shows the creator's
control on one piece of world 1, the ship. The creator's picks were
found pictures of a shuttle, a one-deck cutaway, a cockpit and crew
quarters; we do not show them. From them an agent planned the bridge
alone (v1, a 4.6 by 4.6 m room of 27 pieces). The creator corrected the
plan in words: a smaller cockpit, rooms the game needs, a long flat
wedge rather than a rocket, a nose less pointed, and an outline measured
from drawings rather than shaped from words. Each correction made a new
plan, and the creator accepted the seventh. Every plan is ours and cost
nothing; the two drawings the outline was traced from cost EUR 0.28 and
are not shown. The ship's shape follows found artwork the creator chose
to use: shuttle concept by PAUL (zana), found online.

\subsection{From concept to scene}\label{from-concept-to-scene}

\textbf{Inventory, plan and record.} A coding agent lists every visible
element of the concept as an inventory row (kind, anchor, size, count)
or drops it with a written reason; nothing is made or placed that is not
a row. It draws a dimensioned plan, gated on \texttt{mapped} (every
element a row, a door or a written drop; every door says where it
leads). The scene record is written from the plan, inventory, made
ground and sky, gated on \texttt{game-free} (the record cites nothing
from the game the world came from), \texttt{plan-matched} (the shell
within 5 cm of the plan), \texttt{openings} (every doorway onto a floor,
no view into nothing) and \texttt{seed-matched} (every old entry matched
or dropped with a reason). The \texttt{game-free} gate sees cited
numbers only. On world 1 the records cite nothing, but in 18 of its 28
places the data they are written from still take positions from the
source game (Section 4).

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every place is one OpenUSD stage {[}23{]};
each object, one per real-world object, carries its kind, transform,
collision and each part's library surface. Its layers lie in a fixed
order: a slot for the creator's edit layer on top, then people, sound,
light, motion, effects, crops, machines, settle, ground and the
generated base. The edit layer exists only as that slot, an empty layer
the exporter makes once and never writes again; a test on a two-box
place shows an edit there surviving a regenerated base, but the runner's
assembly does not yet carry a creator's edit from one assembly to the
next. Each place is assembled once, from every layer's accepted output;
its \texttt{layers} gate answers unknown while a needed layer has none,
and an old output never stands in.

\textbf{Models, generation first.} Every object goes the prop route
first: Nano Banana Pro {[}24{]} draws its close-up from its crop of the
concept, Pixal3D {[}17{]} builds it, the surface stage paints it, and a
likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with its close-up.
Only an object whose checks fail is built by a code builder a coding
agent writes, reversing the earlier parts check (the creator's choice,
12 October 2026). On 13 of the hub's objects both orders end the same
(Table 1, Figure 2), but generation first draws a close-up of every
object. Every close-up of eight thin and flat objects failed the shape
check, so code builds carry them. The hatch wheel shows the gain: its
code build's outline overlap was 0.45, a fail, and its generated model's
0.60. Most of world 1's models predate the change and enter as
unverified seeds.

\section{Stages and their gates}\label{stages-and-their-gates}

\subsection{Surfaces: the finish map}\label{surfaces-the-finish-map}

Models give shape only; every part takes one surface from a shared
library of rule-based surfaces, written as ProcFunc functions {[}25{]}
on Infinigen's shaders {[}9{]}, coloured from the place's palette and
worn only where wear has a cause (Appendix D). Gemini 3.1 Pro {[}26{]}
lists the close-up's finishes and names a library surface for each. Nano
Banana Pro draws the close-up again with one flat colour per finish, up
to three times until the map fits the close-up's outline, then draws
unlit renders of the raw model's sides and back against it. SegviGen
{[}27{]} splits the mesh guided by the close-up's map, every map votes
on the faces its view sees, and a part whose second finish holds a fifth
of it fails. Each part is painted from its library surface and baked,
never in the picture's colours, which gave every piece its own rust and
stains (Appendix A).

The bake is checked against the close-up. A finish's surface must lie
within ΔE 32 of its colour there, else a nearer surface of its family is
taken or a new one is proposed to the creator, and the part fails by
name. From the greenhouse's water tank, which the creator found baked as
three near greys and tipped, came two checks: a bake fails when it
darkens a finish the close-up shows lighter, or turns all surfaces into
one grey; and short legs are stretched onto one level plane, a foot over
3 cm off the floor failing. Dirt follows the close-up.

\subsection{Resting}\label{resting}

Loose objects are dropped onto the made ground in a physics simulation;
one that would turn more than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm is a
resting fault of the layout and keeps its laid pose. The settle stage
lays where each object comes to rest as a layer (held objects snapped
onto their holders, a stack onto one support, an object that would rest
worse kept as laid), gated on \texttt{rested} and \texttt{resting} (no
fault that appears with the layer and not without it).

\subsection{Light}\label{light}

Lamp strengths are measured, never copied from the source game. The
light layer places live lamps from the scene record, with the sky, the
sun and the Earth. One paid render per place gives every lamp its own
Cycles light group on the place's views; because light adds up, the look
at any strengths is mixed from these passes without rendering again, and
a free sweep chooses the scale that brings the place's brightness within
1.5 times its concept's, halving any lamp that turns what it lights
white. Outdoors the suns are swept after the lamps. A lamp whose fixture
stops 90 percent of its rays moves out of it, and one behind a clear
lens stands just outside, as Cycles passes no light through a glowing
lens.

\subsection{Motion, effects and sound}\label{motion-effects-and-sound}

\textbf{Motion.} Each mover (a path, a hinge, a spin, a rail, a turn, a
climb) has its kind's speed range cited from real machines, gated on
\texttt{clear} (no mover within 3 cm of a still thing or another mover,
read every quarter second), \texttt{speeds}, \texttt{loops},
\texttt{grounded} and \texttt{covered} (every still prim of a moving
kind says why). \textbf{Effects} are emitters measured against real
footage (photographs of lift-offs, rover films of Martian dust devils),
gated on \texttt{attached}, \texttt{footage}, \texttt{light} (the flame
lights the pad at least twice as brightly as the place's lamps) and
\texttt{outside} (no open-sky particle inside a room). \textbf{Sound:}
every source a place names (room tones, objects, motions, each surface's
steps) gets the world's picked file or the best of eight
MOSS-SoundEffect {[}28{]} takes of its brief, gated on \texttt{sources},
\texttt{level}, \texttt{likeness} (CLAP {[}29{]} score at least 0.30,
the tenth percentile of the world's picks) and \texttt{no\ freesound}.

\subsection{People}\label{people}

People are made like props: a FLUX.2 klein {[}30{]} close-up in SOMA-X's
bind pose, built by Pixal3D, painted by the surface line, over SOMA-X's
body fitted just inside (Gauss-Newton, 24 shape numbers), whose skin
weights each look point takes from its closest triangle; Kimodo's clips
{[}31{]}, from sentences, move it. The \textbf{shell gate} passes a look
when 99 percent of its soft points lie within 5 cm of the body, its
worst point recorded. Hard parts ride one joint, belts are cloth, each
clip gets its own verdict (tucks, hard parts stretched, cloth or body
through cloth) and casts use only passing clips; a far-only look is
judged on its far body (Appendix B). \textbf{Casts:} SAM 3 {[}18{]}
finds the concept's figures, a judge tells people from pictures,
reflections and empty suits, and casts are gated on \texttt{counted},
\texttt{standing}, \texttt{clear}, \texttt{made} and \texttt{drapes}; no
crowd copy fills a tenth of a view's height.

\subsection{Completion}\label{completion}

A place is complete when its gate passes on its final assembly: every
row made or dropped with a reason, nothing unmade, no placeholder or
resting fault, the creator's picks shown, every layer and shown place
present, nothing cited from the source game. \textbf{Named gaps.} A
fault the creator decides to ship with today is written down as a named
gap: who decided, when, why, the objects and their measured faults. It
moves only those objects' faults from the gate where they arise to the
completion gate, which fails the place by name with the numbers; any
other fault still stops the line, and the gate is never loosened. A
cast's person with no accepted body is a named gap the same way. A hook
stops an agent claiming a place is done while its gate fails: agents
judging their own scenes agree with a measured geometry score only 45.8
percent of the time {[}15{]}.

\subsection{Budget and ledgers}\label{budget-and-ledgers}

Machines and Google calls write ledger lines with their node's ticket;
the month's spend is the higher of ledgers and bill, which caught
misses: EUR 1,554 billed against EUR 1,387 in the cloud ledger on 12
October 2026, and EUR 549 billed by Google for a day the earlier ledger
put at EUR 30. Over 13.8 hours of world 1's run the ledgers hold EUR
117.45 of machines and EUR 34.19 of Google calls, most for painting (EUR
37.61), the finish maps (EUR 26.59), the people's bodies (EUR 16.09) and
the light passes (EUR 10.17).

\textbf{Forecast.} Before a paid piece runs, its estimate is written
down with the measured unit cost it rests on (the ledgers' medians per
node, model or picture); after it, the ledger's actual. The projected
total for the month is reported against a hard stop the creator sets.
The record shows which estimates to trust: measured unit costs held (22
close-ups for the ship estimated at EUR 3.10 to 6.20, actual 3.50; seven
box checks 1.75, actual 2.19; the ship's six rooms 27.40, actual about
16.70; its eight shell kits 1.20, actual 0.60), while the runner's flat
table figure overstated work it could carry over (the prologue's
surfaces and light, EUR 101.50 in the table, cost 0.86). The runner's
forecast for a run is a ceiling, not an estimate: the four runs of 15
October were forecast at up to EUR 72.57, 33, 70.50 and 24 and cost
10.13, 10.14, 0.67 and 0.60, the last two stopped early because they ran
older code. One ship run spent EUR 13.58 before it stopped on missing
free inputs; the rule since then is a free pass over every input before
a paid run. The bill runs about 21 percent over the ledgers, so the
forecast multiplies every ledger-priced estimate by 1.21; on 15 October
it put the month at EUR 495 to 507 against the creator's hard stop of
EUR 510.

\textbf{Held and run, 15 October.} Before the night's runs the runner
listed every stale node with its price. All people work was held for
next month, EUR 89.50: 23 bodies (EUR 56.50, 6 to be made again after
changes to the people maker, 17 not made yet), 12 takes (EUR 18), their
maps, names, parts and paint (EUR 14) and one likeness calibration (EUR
1). Three review renders (EUR 14) were left out, as neither the
completion gate nor the films read them. The place films, EUR 0.70 each,
have not run.

\section{Evidence}\label{evidence}

Four worlds are planned; world 1's 28 places are judged once their
layers stand accepted.

\textbf{Layouts from the source game.} The framework's own layout, made
from the plan and the concept, is proven only on the ship's 8 places and
the 2 door kits. The other 18 places still take their layouts from the
source game: 507 entries in their data cite the game, 338 of them
positions, and 7 kits are laid in the game's frame, with 6,432 piece
positions. The module rooms' shells, laid from the game's shell numbers,
are not in these counts. The check sees cited numbers only, so an
uncited copy passes: the launch's accepted scene record still lights the
rocket at the game's position. Each of the 18 places ships this as a
named gap of the completion gate's game check (Table 3); deriving their
layouts again from plan and concept is a rebuild not yet priced.

\textbf{People.} Scored on what is drawn, no clip of any look is clean
(Table 2). Takes in a wider A-pose crossed less at the bind and more in
motion (10,610 visible crossings on the player's clips against 4,078),
with no clip clean in either pose. People therefore ship as named gaps:
the characters layer lays the people whose bodies stand accepted, and
the completion gate fails every missing one by name.

\section{Limitations, future work and
conclusion}\label{limitations-future-work-and-conclusion}

One creator judges; the creator accepted the hub before the runner.
Closed models (Nano Banana Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro) may change or go. Hard
belts failed deep bends; light is live, measured on earlier layouts;
objects are not interactive; 18 of 28 places still take their layouts
from the source game. Next: layouts from plan and concept in every
place, three worlds, an engine adapter, interaction, baked lamps, open
models, locked parts, LEGO-Bench {[}15{]}. In short, SCORE keeps, keys
and judges every stage's output, so a fault is fixed where it happens;
completed places will show if it holds.

Three findings shaped the method. First, deterministic checks catch what
agents miss: in round one, the share of rays leaking out through walls
and roof fell from 1.53 percent of 5,817 to zero, and the colour noise
within surfaces from a median of 31.1 to 3.2 percent, but only walking
through the loaded room showed 26 old models still in it, which became
the made-only check. Second, generated pieces that kept their pictures'
colours made the room inconsistent, so surfaces come only from the
library. Third, generated composite objects melted their parts together,
and generated boxes leaned (one locker by 13 degrees), which led to one
model per real-world object and to the straightness check. By round six
the room used 0.45 million triangles, against 1.96 million in round
four, and 187 MB on disk, against 487 MB.

\section{Check details}\label{check-details}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Room checks} cast rays to find light leaking out through walls
  and roof, find pieces in front of a real opening and roof and wall
  fittings more than 3 cm off their surface or turned more than 5
  degrees from it, and count pieces, triangles and lamps against the
  room's budget.
\item
  \textbf{Door check:} every door must stand in a wall or partition that
  fully separates its two sides, tested by walking a grid from one side
  to the other.
\item
  \textbf{Model check:} every model must have walls at least 3 mm thick.
  A generated box fails if one of its sides tilts more than 2 degrees or
  is warped. Making a generated model solid stops if its bounding box
  grows more than 3 percent.
\item
  \textbf{Likeness gate:} a made model's outline from eight sides must
  overlap its close-up's by at least 0.50, a least set on the hub's
  twelve accepted generated models with a margin of 0.05, and its DINOv2
  {[}16{]} similarity must reach a least set the same way; without that
  calibration the gate answers unknown.
\item
  \textbf{Finish maps:} a close-up's map passes when its outline
  overlaps the close-up's by at least 0.85 and it uses every listed
  finish; a part whose second finish holds 20 percent of its voted area
  mixes finishes and fails.
\item
  \textbf{Bake check:} each baked part within ΔE 20 (CIE76) of its
  surface's colour and within ΔE 32 of its close-up's; lightness order
  fails when the close-up shows one surface more than 10 L* lighter and
  the bake makes it more than 3 L* darker; one look fails when no two
  near-neutral surfaces bake far enough apart for a viewer to tell at
  the place's view distance, while the close-up shows them more than ΔE
  24 apart. What a viewer tells is measured, not set: Stone, Szafir and
  Setlur measured the CIELAB difference half of their viewers see
  between two colours s degrees wide, per axis ND(50, s) = C + K/s, with
  (C, K) = (5.08, 0.75) for L*, (5.34, 1.54) for a* and (5.35, 2.87) for
  b* on sizes 1/3 to 6 degrees {[}32{]}; two surfaces are told apart
  when their difference, each axis over its ND, is 1 or more. A
  surface's size is its model's middle size times the root of its area
  share, seen from the place's nearest view. This replaced a fixed ΔE
  12, which failed the wreck's access cover on a 9.3 L* step between its
  steel and its scorched white that its owner sees (11.3 degrees from
  the nearest view; half of viewers see 5.2 L* at that size); a quadrant
  of a standing model's base more than 3 cm off the floor fails. Glass,
  screens, lamps and print are judged by their own colour and left out
  of the order.
\item
  \textbf{Settling and resting check:} an object that would turn more
  than 15 degrees or drift more than 30 cm when dropped keeps its laid
  pose and is marked as not resting; small debris, with no side over 1.4
  m, may turn freely and drift up to 1 m. Hung objects are exempt by
  their inventory anchor, and fixed objects are not judged for tipping.
  The triage fails a foot off its support and an up axis leaning more
  than 3 degrees.
\item
  \textbf{Light:} the place's median brightness within 1.5 times its
  concept's and every view within 2.5 times; a lamp is blown when over
  20 percent of the pixels where it gives at least half the light are
  white, and unlit when it gives less than 0.001 percent of every view's
  light.
\item
  \textbf{Motion:} no mover within 3 cm of a still thing or another
  mover, read every 0.25 s; rounds join within 1 mm; speeds within each
  kind's cited range.
\item
  \textbf{Sound:} CLAP score against the brief at least 0.30, the tenth
  percentile of 55 of the world's picks; a take no more than 0.5 s
  longer than its brief.
\item
  \textbf{People:} the shell gate passes a look when 99 percent of its
  soft points lie within 5 cm of the fitted body; the worst point and
  its joint are recorded. A clip is usable when no frame tucks more than
  5 mm, no hard part stretches past 1.2 times its bind length, cloth
  crosses cloth no more than in the earlier body's same clip, and no
  part's edges pass through cloth deeper than 3 mm more often than at
  the bind. A far-only look is judged on its joins, likeness and the far
  body's triangles (at most 1,220); the near outfit's triangles and
  shell offset are recorded, not gating. The A-pose outline is judged
  against the finished Pixal3D take the build starts from; the take's
  own loss against the raw model is recorded apart. A crowd copy stands
  only where it fills under a tenth of every view's height.
\item
  \textbf{Worse than accepted:} a measure counts as worse only when it
  moves the wrong way by at least its tolerance, the smallest change a
  viewer could see: one more fault for a count; 0.02 of full scale for
  lightness on 0 to 1 (a halo, fill, beam or haze wash), and 5 of 255
  levels for a colour gap, the step a viewer notices between two
  pictures; 0.05 for a brightness ratio; one percent of the picture for
  a share of it; a twentieth of the Earth's lit disc; 5 mm for a length.
  A score no viewer reads directly, such as a likeness score, is worse
  by any move. Tolerances decide only whether an output waits for the
  creator; a check that fails still fails.
\item
  \textbf{The creator's accept carried forward:} when the creator
  accepts a worse output, that accept records what was allowed, each
  measure's value before and after. A later output that passes every
  gate and is no worse than the output taken, within the tolerances, is
  accepted by the runner with that accept named; a move beyond it still
  waits for the creator.
\item
  \textbf{What the creator acts on:} the creator rejects, accepts or
  waives only an output already seen on its review page. This is the
  creator's rule; the commands do not yet check that the page was
  opened.
\item
  \textbf{People seen by the cameras (experimental):} a clip's crossings
  of body through cloth are counted only where a camera that will show
  the person can see them: the place's views at the person's cast spot,
  and a close front and three-quarter view. Crossings no camera sees are
  recorded, not counted against the clip. So far this is a measure on
  the stored bodies (Section 3.5); the per-clip gate does not use it
  yet.
\item
  \textbf{Completion gate:} the requirements given in Section 3.6, each
  with its result tied to a fingerprint of the stage's files and inputs;
  a result made on another stage counts as unknown. The placeholder
  check judges every mesh a code builder draws, and only the shell
  builders pass unnamed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{People results in detail (experimental, Section 3.5).} The body
gates are joins, outline, likeness, triangles and shell. Nev's and
Sefa's boot tuck bands lie 9 to 11 mm outside the trouser leg at the
bind pose, in every clip before any motion. Scored on drawn surfaces
only, no clip of any look is usable: skin seams open and settled cloth
crosses itself beyond the bind. Bound as one closed mesh, the player's
limbs sink into chest and hips, 87 to 563 crossing points visible from
the places' cameras and close views per clip, the armpit surface
stretching between arm and chest. Where hair or cloth meets the head or
hand skin the two sides are weighted differently and open as the joints
move (cloth seams never open); openings over 10 cm on three looks are
not yet confirmed by a render. Binding the take as one closed mesh
removes the openings. Pushing points out of the look's own volume,
limiting the offset from SOMA-X at the armpits and inner thighs,
fold-centred weights and dual-quaternion skinning each left the visible
crossings the same or higher. A close-up drawn in a wider A-pose (arms
45 degrees below level, feet 35 cm apart) gives a take with fewer
crossings at the bind (the player's 678 against 772) but more in motion,
as the clips bring the arms further down: 10,610 visible crossings
against 4,078 over the player's 19 clips, and no clip clean in either
pose. The clips' joint angles stay within the normal human ranges of the
American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, except hip extension of 24 and
27 degrees (limit 20) in two clips.

\textbf{Decisions recorded in these checks.} Three people's gates are
decisions of the captain, the agent session that runs world 1, taken on
12 and 13 October 2026 under the creator's rules and open to the
creator's reversal. (1) The shell gate counts 99 percent of the soft
points rather than every point, with the worst point recorded: the
points beyond it were loose cloth edges (cuffs, hems, caps), which no
part label names, so they could not be given a joint of their own by
label. (2) A look seen only far away is judged on what is drawn far
away, not on the near outfit it was made from. (3) The A-pose outline is
judged against the finished take, not the raw model, so the take's own
loss is not charged to the build. Earlier, the full-body cut (which
looks get bodies in full and which only far ones) was set by the
people's share of the budget.

\section{The surface library}\label{the-surface-library}

The surface library holds families of variants, among them painted,
steel, aluminium, rubber, plastic, cable, fabric, composite, glass,
screen, light, print, wood, ground, foil, water, skin and hair. After
the hub's second round it held 71 variants made from 14 ProcFunc
recipes; it now holds 369 variants in 26 families made from 22 recipes.
Each variant is a recipe with settings, takes its colours only from the
palette, and is baked into base-colour, metal-roughness and normal maps
at a texture density that depends on how close the player can get. Wear
has three levels and comes from causes: edges, and kicks within 32 cm of
the floor that a piece stands on. A surface the finish-map route
proposes enters an append-only registry and joins the library only when
the creator accepts it.

are models with stray parts below their feet. No agent claimed a place
was complete, so the gate's hook never had to refuse a claim. No row
carries an owner's pick, so the pick lock was not tested.
