\begin{abstract}
Games, film, robotics and embodied AI all require 3D environments that
can be physically simulated, in which every object is a separate body
with its own collision geometry, mass and joints under the gravity of
the ground it stands on, and which remain editable afterwards and owned
by whoever made them. World models generate video frames that no physics
simulator can open, and one-shot 3D generators produce a single fused
scene without separate bodies or clear usage rights. SCORE is a
framework that synthesises such environments. It builds them as film and
game studios build scenes in OpenUSD, the open scene description
standard, with each scene composed inside its parent scene and each kind
of work on it (layout, surfaces, physics, lighting, motion, effects,
sound and people) held in a layer of its own that is checked and changed
independently of the others. A creator fixes the design from the top
down, first an environment concept that sets its visual style and
physical setting, then a concept image for each scene. Open vision
foundation models, procedural generation tools and coding agents produce
every layer below, each output passing measured acceptance tests, called
gates, before the next is made, and every model licenses its output for
commercial use, so the creator remains the author. Every step is
identified by a hash of its inputs, so an edit reruns only the steps
that depend on it. The result is a single OpenUSD environment whose
physics description a simulator reads as written and which Newton steps
with the MuJoCo solver. We describe the framework and its method, with a
first environment as the evidence, whose four demonstrations remain open
until its paid production run, called a batch, has completed. Each
character also receives a simulated body in Newton, driven by the
segment rotations of its SOMA body model. We close with the route
towards subject-specific musculoskeletal bodies, a data loop in which
generated movement, retained only when it is dynamically consistent,
trains controllers that actuate a body's muscles, and layered training
sets with physics-derived ground truth, on which estimators can learn to
infer muscle activations, joint moments and ground reaction forces from
monocular video. Code and paper are at
https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT licence).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Simulated 3D environments serve games, film, robot learning and embodied
AI {[}1{]}. Across these uses they share a common structure of separate
objects with collision geometry, mass and joints, a ground surface with
its gravity, light and sound emitted by identifiable sources, and scenes
that can be reopened, modified and simulated again. Authoring such an
environment by hand is expensive, and a generative system that takes
over this work must leave the result in the hands of its author, since
in the Game Developers Conference's 2026 survey 52 percent of game
workers considered generative AI to be harming the industry {[}2{]}.

World models generate an environment as a sequence of video frames
{[}3{]}, {[}4{]} that no physics simulator can step forward in time. 3D
environment generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} produce geometry, mostly as a
single fused asset from which separate objects have to be recovered
afterwards {[}7{]}, {[}8{]}. Both operate in a single pass, so a fault
in the output cannot be traced to the step that caused it. Procedural
content generation systems {[}9{]}, {[}10{]} and coding agents, language
models that write scenes as executable programs {[}11{]}, {[}12{]},
{[}13{]}, {[}14{]}, {[}15{]}, {[}16{]}, keep the environment explicit
and inspectable, but they either reconstruct a scene that already exists
or assemble it from a fixed asset library. The models they produce often
float above their supports or come apart {[}17{]}, such agents assess
their own geometry unreliably {[}18{]}, and on scenes reconstructed from
video the strongest model scores 0.91 on static scenes but only 0.67 on
dynamic ones {[}19{]}. Open vision foundation models {[}20{]}, large
models pretrained on broad image data and reused across tasks, now
recover 3D shape {[}21{]}, segmentation masks {[}22{]} and depth
{[}23{]} from images, and a coding agent can call them as tools, yet
none of them gives a consistent visual style across objects that
different models produced {[}24{]}, {[}25{]}, and none gives physical
properties.

The components needed for the physics already exist. OpenUSD {[}26{]},
the scene description that Pixar developed for its film production,
stores each department's work in its own layer file and composes scenes
from smaller ones by reference, and its physics schema describes rigid
bodies, colliders, mass and joints within the same stage, the composed
scene. MuJoCo {[}27{]} is a widely used physics engine for articulated
bodies in contact, and Newton {[}28{]}, built on the Warp GPU
programming framework {[}29{]}, runs MuJoCo's solver on the GPU {[}30{]}
and loads OpenUSD scenes directly. What is missing is a route from what
a creator intends to a complete environment in this form.

SCORE is that framework. Its environments are complete, since every
object, surface, light and sound a scene shows exists wherever the
viewer looks. They are owned, because every model in the framework
permits commercial use of its output, so the creator holds the full
rights. They are responsive, because every object carries its collider,
mass and material and every moving part its joints, so a simulator steps
the environment as written. They are editable, since each scene is a
stack of layers produced by steps that are each identified by a key
derived from their inputs, and a change reruns only the steps it
affects. An environment can range from a house on a plot of ground to a
system of worlds that move one another by gravitation.

We contribute the SCORE framework (Section 2), which turns a creator's
locked design into a single OpenUSD environment that is physically
simulable and opens unchanged in a game engine, a robot simulator or a
film renderer. Its method (Section 3) builds a scene from the top down
and layer by layer, with each stage passing measured acceptance tests
before the next begins. The evidence plan (Section 4) comprises four
demonstrations on a first environment, left as gaps until its batch has
run. Section 5 sets out where the framework goes next, towards simulated
people whose bodies are true to the individual person.

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

An environment is stored as a single OpenUSD record. Each scene is one
stage, a composed OpenUSD scene, that sits within its parent scene and
holds its child scenes, each referenced at the room that the parent's
plan, a dimensioned drawing in metres, assigns to it, so that opening a
top scene opens everything below it. Each scene's layers are stacked in
a fixed order of precedence, from a slot reserved for the creator's own
edit layer at the top, through people, sound, light, motion, effects,
crops, machines, physics, settle and ground, down to the base layer laid
out from the locked plan. Each object corresponds to one real-world
object and carries its kind, transform, collider and, for each of its
parts, a surface from the library. The environment's physical setting is
decided in its environment concept (EC), the top-level design the
creator locks, as a plot of ground with its gravity and the path of its
sun, as a single world, or as a system of bodies that move one another
by gravitation. A real celestial body takes its values from a cited
table, and an invented one is specified by its radius, mass, orbit and
spin. The physics layer writes a scene's rigid bodies, masses, joints
and the gravity of the body it stands on in OpenUSD's physics schema, so
that any reader of that schema obtains the same scene.

The creator works in the creator cockpit, a single authenticated web
page in which he locks the design, purchases paid computation and
reviews what the framework reports. The work on an environment is
represented as a table of stages derived from its designs, and the table
for environment 1 has 261 rows. A node is one stage applied to one scene
(written \texttt{assembly@hangar} for the assembly stage of the hangar
scene) or to the environment as a whole, and a single runner produces,
evaluates and accepts every node. Once the creator has approved a
design, it runs without further approval.

\textbf{Keys.} A node is computed only when its key has no completion
record, as in a content-addressed build system. The key is a hash over
the stage and scene, the version of its code, its contracts, parameters
and random seed, the pinned container images of its models, every file
it reads and the accepted output of every node it reads. The code
version covers only code that can shape the output. Code that decides
where and how a job runs changes what a run costs but never what it
produces, and is therefore excluded from every key. An edit or a fixed
fault changes only its own node's key and the keys of the nodes
downstream of it, so only those steps run again. Outputs are stored
under their content hash and never overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate is an automated acceptance test
that returns pass, fail or unknown, together with its measurements and a
reason. Missing evidence counts as unknown, and unknown blocks
acceptance just as fail does. The runner accepts an output automatically
when every gate has passed and the output is no worse than the last
accepted one, and downstream nodes read only accepted outputs. Four
actions are reserved for the creator. He may reject an accepted output,
which marks every node that read it as stale, accept a worse output with
a stated reason, waive one gate for one key, and override the runner for
one paid run. Coding agents are prevented from taking these actions by a
store that refuses such records when they come from an agent's process,
and by a hook in the agent's harness.

\textbf{Batches.} Paid computation is purchased as a batch, a production
run with a single cost estimate. The creator's approval sets the batch's
budget across all of its attempts, and spending guards stop the batch
when that budget is reached. Within a batch every paid job runs once,
and a failed output becomes a batch gap, which the completion gate
lists. Every paid stage is priced from a unit cost measured on the cloud
billing ledgers. A stage that has not yet been measured in a run has no
price, and its estimate is shown separately as a guess.

\section{Method: building an
environment}\label{method-building-an-environment}

\subsection{From concept to plan}\label{from-concept-to-plan}

\textbf{The environment concept.} The creator first locks the EC, which
fixes the environment's visual style and physical setting together with
his reference images and the scenes it contains, in a short structured
interview conducted with the vision-language model Gemini 3.1 Pro
{[}31{]}. An environment plan then places the site of each top scene on
that ground and computes the sky visible from it.

\textbf{A concept for each scene, top down.} A concept image is then
locked for each scene, parents before their child scenes. Each candidate
image is one call to the image generation model Nano Banana Pro
{[}32{]}, made on a concept review page where the creator marks regions
and requests a new image. A child scene is drawn only once its parent
has been locked and planned, from a crop of the parent's concept, so
that it fits the parent it stands in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} Gemini 3.1 Pro lists each object of the
locked concept as a row of the scene inventory, giving its kind, count,
anchor and size in metres, and the segmentation model SAM 3 {[}22{]}
localises each row with a bounding box. Nothing outside the inventory is
generated or placed. Nano Banana Pro then draws the scene from directly
above, and Gemini 3.1 Pro writes the plan in metres from that drawing,
the concept and the inventory. The plan gives the outline, walls,
openings, levels, stairs, roofs and terrain, each item with its
orientation, people as the spots on which they stand, and the rooms of
the child scenes. Free checks, which make no paid model call, test that
every row is placed as many times as its count, inside the outline and
clear of other floor items, that every door and standing spot can be
reached on foot, and that the child scenes tile their parent. Any
remaining violations are returned to the model as notes at most twice,
and only what still fails is recorded as a gap. The creator's edits on
the plan review page always take precedence and cause everything that
follows to be recomputed.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route follows one coarse-to-fine loop at
every scale. A parent scene receives its concept, plan and a rough block
model, and its plan is divided into the shapes of its child scenes. Once
those shapes are fixed, all child scenes are produced in parallel, each
repeating the loop. The stages \texttt{rough-model} and
\texttt{shell-split} are implemented but have not yet run.

\subsection{Layers from row kinds}\label{layers-from-row-kinds}

A scene's layers are derived from its design alone. Each inventory row's
kind, determined from the noun in the inventory reading by a single
table of rules, states what its objects do, whether they emit, move, lie
loose, glow, grow, act or sound. A scene receives an effects, motion,
physics, crops or people layer only for rows that do so, and always a
light and a sound layer. Each layer is generated from its rows by the
rule of their kind, so a new environment needs no hand-written list of
layers. The scene record holds what no generated object covers (building
shells, stairs, ground and water, the distant backdrop, lamps and the
neighbouring scenes its doorways lead to), and its gate requires the
shell to lie within 5 cm of the plan and every doorway to open onto a
floor.

\textbf{Models.} Objects follow the prop route first, which turns a
single image of an object into a 3D model. Nano Banana Pro draws a
close-up of the object from its crop of the concept and redraws it at
most three times when the shape check rejects it. The image-to-3D model
Pixal3D {[}21{]} then reconstructs the mesh, and a likeness gate
compares its silhouettes from eight viewpoints with the close-up. Only
an object whose checks fail is built instead by a procedural builder, a
program that a coding agent writes, and a thin straight object (a pipe,
post or beam) whose every close-up failed is generated in code by
sweeping its cross-section along its length.

\textbf{Surfaces.} Everything a scene shows comes from the creator's
asset library, score\_lib, or is recorded as a written exception. A
generated model contributes geometry only, and each of its parts takes
one material from a library of procedural materials, written as ProcFunc
functions {[}33{]} on Infinigen's shaders {[}9{]} and coloured from the
scene's palette. The finish route labels each face of the model from
free renders, matches each finish to a library material, bakes it into
textures and checks the bake against the close-up, requiring each finish
to lie within a colour difference of ΔE 32 of its colour there. A free
library check traces every material, model, light, sound and character
of an accepted assembly back to the library or to an exception.

\textbf{Settle and physics.} Loose objects are dropped onto the
generated ground in a rigid-body simulation, the settle, and an object
that would rotate by more than 15 degrees or drift by more than 30 cm is
recorded as a resting fault and keeps its laid-out pose. The settle
writes the pose in which each object comes to rest as a layer. The
physics trial then steps the composed scene in Newton, using MuJoCo's
solver with Newton's contact handling under the gravity of the body the
scene stands on, and evaluates it with the gates \texttt{reacted},
\texttt{at\ rest}, \texttt{energy} (no increase in the energy of
undriven bodies) and \texttt{hinge} (a hinged door leaf swings to its
joint limit). The trial moved from Blender's Bullet engine {[}34{]} to
Newton on 27 October 2026. On a test scene at lunar gravity (two dropped
crates, a driven door with a wheel fixed to it and a pushed stool), both
engines gave the same result on every gate, and Newton brought the
crates to rest on their targets where Bullet left them standing off by
its contact margin of 8 mm. Newton's deterministic mode, which
reproduced bitwise-identical results over six runs of that test scene,
cannot handle scenes with many loose bodies, because its buffers exceed
the 32-bit limit, so the trial and the settle run without it. Two runs
of a 25-body test scene then agree within 5e-7 m for every body except
one that falls off the edge of the floor. The settle moves to Newton in
the same way, so that one engine steps both what comes to rest and what
is tested, and one effect is simulated in Newton as a first case for the
effects layer.

\textbf{Light, motion, effects and sound.} The sky is computed from the
physical setting in the EC, never drawn, with each celestial body at its
true direction, apparent size and phase as seen from the scene's site.
The lamps are the inventory's glowing rows. One render per scene places
each lamp in its own light group, and a free sweep mixes their
intensities to bring the scene's brightness within a factor of 1.5 of
its concept's. Every object of a moving row moves by the rule of its
kind at a speed taken from cited real machines, and is gated on nothing
coming within 3 cm of anything else. Emitters are measured against real
footage. Each sound source receives a library file or one take generated
by the text-to-audio model MOSS-SoundEffect {[}35{]}, gated on an
audio-text similarity score from CLAP {[}36{]} of at least 0.30.

\textbf{People.} People are models like any other, each made from a
close-up per person row, reconstructed by Pixal3D and given its
materials by the finish route. The SOMA parametric body model {[}37{]},
estimated from the close-up by SAM 3D Body {[}38{]}, is fitted beneath
the model as its skeletal rig and animated with motion clips from Kimodo
{[}39{]}. A shell gate accepts a clothed appearance when 99 percent of
the points of its non-rigid parts lie within 5 cm of the body. Each
person stands on a person spot of its scene's plan.

\textbf{Sim bodies.} Each character also receives a sim body, its
physical twin in Newton, so that the people in an environment are
physically simulated as well as animated. The minimal body is an
articulated rigid-body model built directly from SOMA's 23 segments,
with a ball joint at each SOMA joint frame. It is driven by the
character's SOMA segment rotations without scaling or per-person setup,
its mass is distributed according to its segment volumes, and it stands
on a SCORE floor so that its ground reaction forces arise from contact.
Muscles are added as a minimal first step. Built this way, the body
follows SOMA's rotations to within 6.9e-9 m, and in Newton it matches
plain MuJoCo to within 3.4e-8 after 200 steps.

\textbf{Completion.} A scene is complete when its completion gate passes
on its final assembly, with every inventory row made or dropped with a
reason, nothing left unmade, no resting fault, and every layer and every
visible scene present. A fault the creator chooses to ship with is an
accepted gap, recorded with who decided, when and why. The gate lists
every gap by name and is never relaxed. A hook prevents a coding agent
from claiming that a scene is done while its gate fails, because agents
judging their own scenes agree with a measured geometry score only 45.8
percent of the time {[}18{]}. The films of a complete scene are rendered
from scripts the creator has locked.

\section{Future work: people true to the
person}\label{future-work-people-true-to-the-person}

The minimal sim body is a starting point, and what follows describes
goals rather than results.

\textbf{Bodies true to the person.} The sim body is to become a full
musculoskeletal model that is specific to the individual, older people
and people with a curved spine included, rather than a scaled generic
template. Joint centres are to be derived from bony landmarks on the
SOMA mesh, followed by segment masses, muscles, joint ranges of motion,
the spine and bone geometry, with each input that SOMA does not provide
marked as assumed and given a plausible range. A full-body
musculoskeletal model with 416 muscles already runs in Newton but still
drifts 1.5 cm from plain MuJoCo after 300 steps, so Newton is first to
be validated for such bodies. The body's joint angles, and then its net
joint moments and ground reaction forces, are to be validated against
OpenCap's LabValidation data.

\textbf{Scenes from motion.} Motion is to come from the SOMA ecosystem,
either estimated from video with GEM-X {[}41{]} or generated from text
and constraints with Kimodo {[}39{]}, in practically unlimited quantity.
The terrain a motion implies becomes a SCORE scene, so the movement is
kept in its setting without the source video or any information that
identifies a person. The sim body then follows each movement as a
prescribed trajectory on the scene's ground, and the simulation solves
for the ground reaction forces from contact, the net joint moments, and
the activations and forces of a 416-muscle model that reproduce it. Any
force and moment that neither the ground contact nor the muscles can
supply remains as the residual at the pelvis. It is recorded for every
frame and never applied to the body, so it quantifies how dynamically
consistent the movement is, and only movements with a small residual are
kept. Biomechanics reduces and reports the same residual to assess
recorded motion {[}42{]}, {[}43{]}, and PhysibleMDM {[}44{]} minimises
an inverse-dynamics residual to make generated motion feasible for a
torque-driven robot; we apply the criterion to generated motion in a
muscle-driven body.

\textbf{Layered training sets.} The same simulations yield layered
training sets. Each sample pairs a rendered image of a clothed person in
a complete scene with physics-derived ground truth for every layer
beneath it, namely the SOMA body and its joint angles, the ground
reaction force under each foot from simulated contact, the muscle
activations and forces, the residual, and the depth, segmentation and
layout of the scene. Their main purpose is to train estimators that
infer muscle activations, joint moments and ground reaction forces
directly from monocular video, quickly enough for routine clinical use.
Recorded data cannot provide this supervision at scale, because muscle
forces cannot be measured non-invasively and force plates and
electromyography confine measurement to a laboratory, whereas the
simulation can generate the bodies, movements and settings an estimator
has to cover, including those of a specific population, so that
domain-specific estimators can be trained for it. BioHuman10M {[}45{]}
already pairs video, part of it rendered, with the activations of a
112-muscle model and with ground reaction forces predicted by a learned
model from recorded motion. Our sets would differ in scenes generated
for the purpose under varied lighting, terrain and clothing, in ground
reaction forces from contact with each scene's own ground, in a
416-muscle model and in a residual for every sample, and their value
depends on whether estimators trained on them transfer to real
force-plate and electromyography recordings.

\textbf{Brains.} A brain is a trained controller that actuates a sim
body's muscles, and its role is to extend the training data beyond what
a motion generator produces. Trained on the retained movements together
with the muscle forces that produce them, it is placed in SCORE's
synthesised worlds under their own gravity and terrain, where it is to
adapt to structures no recording contains, and each run it drives adds
movement to the training sets. Muscle-driven controllers such as KINESIS
{[}46{]} and MuscleMimic {[}47{]} are trained on motion capture, as are
torque-driven ones such as PHC {[}48{]}. To our knowledge, no
muscle-driven controller has yet been trained on generated motion
selected by its residual, and the claim holds only once such a
controller is compared with one trained on motion capture and with one
trained on the same generated motion unfiltered.

\textbf{The data loop.} The amount of data the loop produces scales with
the compute put behind it, since every scene, movement and run adds
samples. This is why SCORE is open, so that others can apply their own
compute to the same framework and share the resulting datasets.
Estimators specialised for clinical use would be trained outside SCORE,
on data that never enters it.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}\label{limitations-and-conclusion}

A single creator judges the results, and he has not yet accepted a run.
Closed models (Nano Banana Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro) draw the concepts and
close-ups and read and plan every scene, and may change or be withdrawn.
The design step, the layers derived from row kinds, the finish route,
the shapes-first loop and the scene tree have not yet carried a scene
through a batch. The edit layer is so far only a reserved slot. The
settle, the effects and the sim body do not yet run in Newton as stages,
and no game engine or robot simulator has yet opened the result. Next
come environment 1's batch with its four demonstrations, an engine
adapter, the edit layer carried across assemblies, further environments,
open models replacing the closed ones, and the data loop, in which
residual-filtered generated movement trains muscle-driven controllers
whose value a comparison with motion capture has yet to show. SCORE
synthesises an environment as studios build one, scene by scene and
layer by layer, in a form a simulator steps as written, and an accepted
environment will show whether the approach holds.
