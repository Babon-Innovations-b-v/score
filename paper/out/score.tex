\author{J.P. Kardolus, Babon Innovations B.V., Utrecht, The Netherlands}
\date{Working draft, 27 October 2026}

\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Games, film, robotics and embodied AI all need 3D environments that can
be simulated, in which every object is its own body with collision, mass
and joints under the gravity of its ground, editable afterwards and
owned by whoever made it. World models give frames no simulator opens,
and one-shot 3D generators give one fused scene without separate bodies
or clear rights. SCORE is a framework that synthesises such
environments. It builds them as studios build scenes in OpenUSD, each
scene inside its parent scene and each kind of work on it (layout,
surfaces, physics, light, motion, effects, sound and people) in a layer
of its own, checked and changed apart from the rest. A creator locks the
design from the top down, first an environment concept with its style
and physics, then a concept for each scene. Open vision foundation
models, procedural tools and coding agents make every layer below, each
checked by measured gates before the next is made, and every model
allows commercial use of its output, so the creator stays the author.
Every step is keyed on its inputs, so an edit reruns only the steps
behind it. The result is one OpenUSD environment whose physics a
simulator reads as written and Newton steps with MuJoCo's solver. We
describe the framework and its method, with a first environment as the
evidence, whose four demonstrations remain gaps until its batch has run.
Each character also gets a sim body on Newton, driven by its SOMA
segment rotations. We close with the route toward bodies true to the
person, a data loop in which generated movement, kept only when
physically possible, trains models that drive a body's muscles, and
layered training sets that pair rendered people with their forces and
muscle activity, from which fast models can learn to read muscle metrics
from ordinary video. Code and paper are at
https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT licence).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Simulated 3D environments serve games, film, robot learning and embodied
AI {[}1{]}. They share a structure of separate objects with collision,
mass and joints, ground with a gravity, light and sound that come from
somewhere, and scenes that can be opened, changed and simulated again.
Building one by hand is expensive, and a generator that takes the work
over must leave the result in its maker's hands, since in GDC's 2026
survey 52 percent of game workers thought generative AI was hurting the
industry {[}2{]}.

World models generate an environment as frames {[}3{]}, {[}4{]} that no
simulator can step. 3D environment generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} produce
geometry, mostly as one fused asset from which separate objects are
recovered afterwards {[}7{]}, {[}8{]}. Both work in one shot, so a fault
cannot be traced to its step. Procedural systems {[}9{]}, {[}10{]} and
coding agents that write scenes as programs {[}11{]}, {[}12{]},
{[}13{]}, {[}14{]}, {[}15{]}, {[}16{]} keep the environment explicit but
rebuild a scene that already exists or draw from a fixed library. Their
models often float or come apart {[}17{]}, such agents judge their own
geometry unreliably {[}18{]}, and on scenes rebuilt from video the best
model scores 0.91 on static scenes but 0.67 on dynamic ones {[}19{]}.
Open vision foundation models {[}20{]} now read shapes {[}21{]}, masks
{[}22{]} and depth {[}23{]} from pictures, and a coding agent can
connect them as tools, yet none gives one style across objects that
different models made {[}24{]}, {[}25{]}, and none gives physics.

The pieces for the physics exist. OpenUSD {[}26{]}, the scene
description Pixar made for its films, keeps each department's work in
its own layer file and assembles scenes from smaller ones by reference,
and its physics schema states bodies, colliders, mass and joints in the
same stage. MuJoCo {[}27{]} is a widely used solver for articulated
bodies with contact, and Newton {[}28{]}, built on Warp {[}29{]}, runs
MuJoCo's solver on the GPU {[}30{]} and reads OpenUSD directly. What is
missing is a route from what a creator wants to a complete environment
in this form.

SCORE is that framework. Its environments are complete, since every
object, surface, light and sound a scene shows exists wherever the
viewer looks. They are owned, because every model in the framework
allows commercial use of its output, so the creator holds the full
rights. They are responsive, because every object has its collider, mass
and material and what moves has its joints, so a simulator steps the
environment as written. They are editable, since each scene is a stack
of layers made by keyed steps and a change reruns only what it touches.
An environment can be a house on a plot of ground or a system of worlds
that move one another.

We contribute the SCORE framework (Section 2), which turns a creator's
locked design into one OpenUSD environment that is physically simulable
and opens unchanged in a game engine, a robot simulator or a film
renderer. Its method (Section 3) builds a scene top down and layer by
layer, each stage behind measured gates. The evidence plan (Section 4)
comprises four demonstrations on a first environment, left as gaps until
its batch has run. Section 5 sets out where the framework goes next,
toward simulated people true to the person.

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

An environment is one OpenUSD record. Each scene is one stage that sits
within its parent scene and holds its child scenes, referenced at the
rooms its plan gives them, so opening a top scene opens everything below
it. Each scene's layers stack in a fixed order, from a slot for the
creator's edit layer on top through people, sound, light, motion,
effects, crops, machines, physics, settle, ground and the base laid from
the locked plan. Each object, one per real-world object, carries its
kind, transform, collider and each part's library surface. The
environment's physics is decided in its environment concept (EC) as a
plot of ground with a gravity and a sun path, one world, or a system of
bodies that move one another, where a real body takes its values from a
cited table and an invented one is given by its radius, mass, orbit and
spin. The physics layer writes a scene's bodies, mass, joints and the
gravity of the body it stands on in OpenUSD's physics schema, so any
reader of that schema gets the same scene.

The creator works in the creator cockpit, one signed-in web page where
he locks the design, buys paid work and reviews what the framework
reports. An environment is a table of stages read off its designs, and
environment 1's has 261 rows. A node is one stage for one scene
(\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the environment, and one runner makes,
judges and accepts every node. An approved design then runs without
further approval.

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record.
The key hashes the stage and scene, its code version, contracts,
parameters and seed, the pinned model images, every file it reads and
the accepted output of every node it reads. The code version counts only
code that can shape the output. Code deciding where and how a job runs
changes what a run costs, never what it makes, and is in no key. An edit
or a fixed fault moves only its own key and those after it, so only
those steps run again. Outputs are kept by content hash and never
overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown,
with measures and a reason. Missing evidence is unknown, and unknown
blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate
passed and it is no worse than the last accepted one, and later nodes
read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps four actions. He may
reject an accepted output (every node that read it is then stale),
accept a worse one with a reason, waive one gate for one key, and
override the runner for one paid run. Agents are kept from these by a
store that refuses such records from an agent's process and by a hook.

\textbf{Batches.} Paid work is bought as a batch with one estimate. The
creator's approval sets the batch's budget across all its tries, and the
money guards stop it there. Inside a batch every paid job runs once, and
a failed output becomes a batch gap that the completion gate lists.
Every paid stage is priced from a unit cost measured on the ledgers. A
stage with no measured run has no price, and its estimate is shown apart
as a guess.

\section{Method: building an
environment}\label{method-building-an-environment}

\subsection{From concept to plan}\label{from-concept-to-plan}

\textbf{The environment concept.} The creator first locks the EC, its
style and physics together with his reference pictures and the scenes it
holds, in a short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro {[}31{]}. An environment
plan then places each top scene's site on that ground and works out the
sky it sees.

\textbf{A concept for each scene, top down.} A concept is then locked
for each scene, parents before their child scenes. Each draw is one Nano
Banana Pro {[}32{]} call, on a concept review page where he marks areas
and asks for a new draw. A child scene is drawn only once its parent is
locked and planned, on a crop of the parent's concept, so that it fits
the parent it stands in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} Gemini 3.1 Pro lists each object of the
locked concept as a scene inventory row, with its kind, count, anchor
and size in metres, and SAM 3 {[}22{]} boxes each row. Nothing outside
the rows is made or placed. Nano Banana Pro then draws the scene from
straight above and Gemini 3.1 Pro writes the plan in metres from that
drawing, the concept and the inventory. It gives the outline, walls,
openings, levels, stairs, roofs and terrain, each item with its facing,
people as the spots they stand on, and the child scenes' rooms. Free
checks test that every row is placed as often as it counts, inside the
outline and clear of other floor items, that every door and standing
spot is reached on foot, and that the child scenes tile their parent.
What remains goes back as notes at most twice, and only what is left is
a gap. The creator's edits on the plan review page always win and redo
what follows.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route is one loop at every scale. A parent
scene gets its concept, plan and a rough model, and its plan is split
into the shapes of its child scenes. Once those shapes are fixed, every
child scene is made in parallel and repeats the loop. The rows
\texttt{rough-model} and \texttt{shell-split} are built and have not
run.

\subsection{Layers from row kinds}\label{layers-from-row-kinds}

A scene's layers are read off its design alone. Each inventory row's
kind, found from the reading's noun by one table of rules, says what its
objects do (emit, move, lie loose, glow, grow, act or sound). A scene
gets an effects, motion, physics, crops or people layer only for rows
that do so, and always a light and a sound layer. Each layer is made
from its rows by their kind's rule, so a new environment needs no
hand-written list. The scene record holds what no made piece covers
(shells, stairs, ground and water, backdrop, lamps and neighbours),
gated on the shell lying within 5 cm of the plan and every doorway
opening onto a floor.

\textbf{Models.} Objects take the prop route first. Nano Banana Pro
draws its close-up from its crop of the concept and redraws it at most
three times when the shape check rejects it. Pixal3D {[}21{]} builds the
model, and a likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with
the close-up. Only an object whose checks fail is built by a code
builder a coding agent writes, and a thin straight thing (a pipe, post
or beam) whose every close-up failed is swept along its length in code.

\textbf{Surfaces.} Everything a scene shows comes from the creator's
library, score\_lib, or is a written exception. A model gives shape
only, and each part takes one surface from a library of rule-based
surfaces, written as ProcFunc functions {[}33{]} on Infinigen's shaders
{[}9{]} and coloured from the scene's palette. The finish route labels
each face from free renders of the model, matches each finish to a
library surface, bakes it and checks the bake against the close-up, with
each finish within ΔE 32 of its colour there. A free library check
traces every material, model, light, sound and character of an accepted
assembly to the library or an exception.

\textbf{Settle and physics.} Loose objects are dropped onto the made
ground, and one that would turn more than 15 degrees or drift more than
30 cm is a resting fault and keeps its laid pose. The settle writes
where each object rests as a layer. The physics trial then steps the
composed scene on Newton, with MuJoCo's solver on Newton's contacts
under the gravity of the body the scene stands on, and judges it by the
gates \texttt{reacted}, \texttt{at\ rest}, \texttt{energy} (no rise in
undriven energy) and \texttt{hinge} (a hinged leaf swings to its limit).
The trial moved from Blender's Bullet {[}34{]} to Newton on 27 October
2026. On a toy scene at Moon gravity (two crate drops, a driven door
with a wheel fixed to it, a pushed stool), both engines gave the same
answers on every gate, and Newton landed the crates on their targets
where Bullet left its contact margins of 8 mm. Newton's deterministic
mode, bitwise equal over six runs on that toy, cannot hold scenes with
many loose bodies (its buffers pass the 32-bit limit), so the trial and
the settle run without it. Two runs of a 25-body toy then agree within
5e-7 m on every body but one that falls off the floor's edge. The settle
moves to Newton the same way, so one engine steps what comes to rest and
what is tried, and one effect is stepped on Newton as a first case for
the effects layer.

\textbf{Light, motion, effects and sound.} The sky is worked out from
the EC's physics, never drawn, with each body at its true direction,
size and phase as seen from the scene's site. The lamps are the
inventory's glowing rows. One render per scene gives each lamp its own
light group, and a free sweep mixes their strengths to bring the scene
within 1.5 times its concept's brightness. Every object of a moving row
moves by its kind's rule at a speed cited from real machines, gated on
nothing coming within 3 cm of another thing. Emitters are measured
against real footage. Each sound source gets a library file or one
MOSS-SoundEffect {[}35{]} take, gated on a CLAP {[}36{]} score of at
least 0.30.

\textbf{People.} People are models like any other, each made from a
close-up per person row, built by Pixal3D and painted by the finish
route. SOMA's body {[}37{]}, read from the close-up by SAM 3D Body
{[}38{]}, is fitted under it as its rig and moved by Kimodo's clips
{[}39{]}. A shell gate passes a look when 99 percent of its soft points
lie within 5 cm of the body. Each person stands on a person spot of its
scene's plan.

\textbf{Sim bodies.} Each character also gets a sim body, its physical
twin on Newton, so people in an environment are simulated as well as
animated. The minimal body is built straight from SOMA's 23 segments
with a ball joint at each SOMA joint frame. It is driven by the
character's SOMA segment rotations without scaling or per-person setup,
its mass spread by its segment volumes, and stands on a SCORE floor so
its ground forces come from contact. Muscles enter as a minimal first
step. Built this way, the body follows SOMA's rotations to within 6.9e-9
m, and on Newton it matches plain MuJoCo to 3.4e-8 after 200 steps.

\textbf{Completion.} A scene is complete when its gate passes on its
final assembly, with every row made or dropped with a reason, nothing
unmade, no resting fault, and every layer and shown scene present. A
fault the creator ships with is an accepted gap, written with who
decided, when and why. The gate lists every gap by name and is never
loosened. A hook stops an agent claiming a scene is done while its gate
fails, as agents judging their own scenes agree with a measured geometry
score only 45.8 percent of the time {[}18{]}. A complete scene's films
are made from scripts the creator locked.

\section{Future work: people true to the
person}\label{future-work-people-true-to-the-person}

The minimal sim body is a start, and what follows are goals rather than
results.

\textbf{Bodies true to the person.} The sim body is to become fully
musculoskeletal and true to the person, older people and a curved spine
included, instead of a resized template. Joint centres are to come from
bone landmarks on its SOMA mesh, then mass, muscles, joint ranges, spine
and bone shape, each input SOMA lacks marked as assumed with a range. A
full musculoskeletal body with 416 muscles already runs on Newton but
still drifts 1.5 cm from plain MuJoCo after 300 steps, so Newton is to
be validated for bodies first. Its joint angles, then its joint moments
and ground forces, are to be held to OpenCap's LabValidation data.

\textbf{Scenes from motion.} Motion is to come from the SOMA ecosystem,
with GEM-X {[}41{]} reading it from video, Kimodo {[}39{]} making it
from sentences without limit, and sequences joined from datasets of
both. The terrain a motion implies becomes a SCORE scene, so the scene
keeps the motion without video or anything identifying a person. A sim
body follows each movement exactly on its scene's ground, and physics
computes the ground contact, the joint torques and the forces of the 416
muscles the movement needs. What neither the ground nor the muscles can
supply is the residual, recorded for every frame and never applied, so
it measures how physically possible the movement is, and only movement
with a small residual is kept. Biomechanics already reduces and reports
this residual as a measure of the quality of recorded motion {[}42{]},
{[}43{]}, and PhysibleMDM {[}44{]} lowers an inverse-dynamics residual
to make generated motion feasible for a torque-driven robot. We apply
the same check to generated motion played through a muscle-driven body.

\textbf{Layered training sets.} The same runs can yield layered training
sets. Each sample is a rendered picture of a clothed person in a full
scene, paired with exact values for every layer beneath it, from the
SOMA body and joint angles, the ground force under each foot from
simulated contact and the muscle activations and forces to the residual,
depth, segmentation and the scene's layout. Their main use is to train
fast models that read muscle activity, joint moments and ground forces
from ordinary video. No recorded dataset can teach this at scale, since
muscle forces cannot be measured directly in people and force plates and
muscle sensors confine recording to a laboratory, whereas the simulation
can generate the bodies, movements and settings a model must cover,
including those of a particular group, so that a model can be
specialised to it. BioHuman10M {[}45{]} already pairs video, rendered
clips among it, with the activations of a 112-muscle body and with
ground forces estimated by a learned model from recorded motion. Our
sets would differ in scenes made for the purpose in any light, ground
and clothing, in forces from contact with each scene's own ground, in a
416-muscle body and in a residual for every sample, and their worth
rests on a model trained on them carrying over to real force-plate and
muscle recordings.

\textbf{Brains.} A brain is a trained model that drives a sim body's
muscles so it moves on its own, and its role here is to widen the data
beyond what a motion generator offers. Trained on the kept movements
together with the muscle forces that produce them, it is placed in
SCORE's synthesised worlds under their own gravity and ground, where it
is to adapt to structures no recording holds, and every run it drives
adds movement for the training sets. Muscle-driven controllers such as
KINESIS {[}46{]} and MuscleMimic {[}47{]} learn from motion capture, as
torque-driven ones such as PHC {[}48{]} do. To our knowledge, no
muscle-driven controller has yet been trained on generated motion
filtered by its residual, and the claim holds only once such a brain is
compared with one trained on motion capture and with one trained on the
same generated motion unfiltered.

\textbf{The data loop.} Models specialised for clinical use would be
trained outside SCORE on data that never enters it. The value of the
loop grows with the compute put behind it, since every scene, movement
and run adds data, which is why SCORE is open, so that others can put
their own compute behind the same framework and share the sets they make
back.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}\label{limitations-and-conclusion}

One creator judges, and he has not yet accepted a run. Closed models
(Nano Banana Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro) draw the concepts and close-ups and
read and plan every scene, and may change or be withdrawn. The design
step, the layers from row kinds, the finish route, the shapes-first loop
and the scene tree have not yet carried a scene through a batch. The
edit layer is a slot. The settle, the effects and the sim body are not
yet on Newton, and no game engine or robot simulator has yet opened the
result. Next come environment 1's batch with its four demonstrations, an
engine adapter, the edit layer carried across assemblies, more
environments, open models replacing the closed ones, and the data loop,
in which residual-filtered generated movement trains brains whose worth
a comparison with motion capture has yet to show. SCORE synthesises an
environment as studios build one, scene by scene and layer by layer, in
a form a simulator steps as written, and an accepted environment will
show whether it holds.
