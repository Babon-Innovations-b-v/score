\begin{abstract}
Games, film, robotics and embodied AI all need 3D environments that can
be simulated: every object its own body with collision, mass and joints,
under the gravity of the ground it stands on, editable afterwards and
owned by whoever made it. World models give frames no simulator opens,
and one-shot 3D generators give one fused scene without separate bodies
or clear rights. SCORE is a framework that synthesizes such
environments. It builds them the way studios build scenes in OpenUSD: a
scene sits within its parent scene, and each kind of work on it (layout,
surfaces, physics, light, motion, effects, sound, people) is a layer of
its own that is checked and changed apart from the rest. A creator locks
the design from the top down, an environment concept with its style and
its physics and then a concept for each scene; open vision foundation
models, procedural tools and coding agents make every layer below, each
checked by measured gates before the next is made, and every model
allows commercial use of its output, so the creator stays the author.
Every step is keyed on its inputs, so an edit runs again only the steps
behind it. The result is one OpenUSD environment whose physics a
simulator reads as written; Newton, with MuJoCo's solver, steps it. We
describe the framework and its method, with a first environment as the
evidence; its four demonstrations are marked gaps until its batch has
run. Each character also gets a sim body on Newton, driven by its SOMA
segment rotations. We close with the route toward bodies true to the
person and trained models that drive them. Code and paper are available
at https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT license).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Simulated 3D environments are raw material for games, film, robot
learning and embodied AI {[}1{]}. What these uses share is not a look
but a structure: separate objects with collision, mass and joints,
ground with a gravity, light and sound that come from somewhere, and a
scene that can be opened, changed and simulated again. Building one by
hand is expensive, and a generator that takes the work over has to leave
the result in its maker's hands: in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game
workers thought generative AI was hurting the industry {[}2{]}.

World models generate an environment as frames {[}3{]}, {[}4{]} that no
simulator can step. 3D environment generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} produce
geometry, mostly as one fused asset from which separate objects are
recovered after the fact {[}7{]}, {[}8{]}. Both work in one shot, so a
fault cannot be traced to the step behind it. Procedural systems
{[}9{]}, {[}10{]} and coding agents that write scenes as programs
{[}11{]}, {[}12{]}, {[}13{]}, {[}14{]}, {[}15{]}, {[}16{]} keep the
environment explicit, but rebuild a scene that already exists or draw
from a fixed library; their models often float or come apart {[}17{]},
such agents judge their own geometry unreliably {[}18{]}, and on scenes
rebuilt from video the best model scores 0.91 on static scenes but 0.67
on dynamic ones {[}19{]}. Open vision foundation models {[}20{]} now
read shapes {[}21{]}, masks {[}22{]} and depth {[}23{]} out of pictures,
and a coding agent can connect them as tools; none gives one style
across objects that different models made {[}24{]}, {[}25{]}, and none
gives physics.

The pieces for the physics exist. OpenUSD {[}26{]}, the scene
description Pixar made for its films, layers each department's work in a
file of its own and assembles scenes from smaller ones by reference, and
its physics schema states bodies, colliders, mass and joints in the same
stage. MuJoCo {[}27{]} is a widely used solver for articulated bodies
with contact; Newton {[}28{]}, built on Warp {[}29{]}, runs MuJoCo's
solver on the GPU {[}30{]} and reads OpenUSD directly. What is missing
is a way to get from what a creator wants to a complete environment in
that form.

SCORE is that framework. Its environments are \textbf{complete}: every
object, surface, light and sound a scene shows exists wherever the
viewer looks. They are \textbf{owned}: every model in the framework
allows commercial use of its output, so the creator holds the full
rights. They are \textbf{responsive}: every object has its collider,
mass and material, and what moves has its joints, so a simulator steps
the environment as written. And they are \textbf{editable}: each scene
is a stack of layers, each layer made by a keyed step, so a change runs
again only what it touches. An environment can be a house on a plot of
ground or a system of worlds that move one another.

We contribute \textbf{the SCORE framework} (Section 2), which turns a
creator's locked design into one OpenUSD environment that is physically
simulable and opens unchanged in a game engine, a robot simulator or a
film renderer; \textbf{its method} (Section 3), the stages that build a
scene top down and layer by layer, each behind measured gates; and
\textbf{the evidence plan} (Section 4), four demonstrations on a first
environment, left as gaps until its batch has run. Section 5 sets out
where the framework goes next, toward simulated people true to the
person.

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

An environment is one OpenUSD record. Each \textbf{scene} is one stage;
a scene sits within its parent scene and holds its child scenes,
referenced at the rooms its plan gives them, so opening a top scene
opens everything below it. Each scene's layers stack in a fixed order: a
slot for the creator's edit layer on top, then people, sound, light,
motion, effects, crops, machines, physics, settle, ground and the base
laid from the locked plan. Each object, one per real-world object,
carries its kind, transform, collider and each part's library surface.
The environment's \textbf{physics} is decided in its environment concept
(EC): a plot of ground with a gravity and a sun path, one world, or a
system of bodies that move one another, a real body taking its values
from a cited table and an invented one given by its radius, mass, orbit
and spin. The physics layer writes a scene's bodies, mass, joints and
the gravity of the body it stands on in OpenUSD's physics schema, so any
reader of that schema gets the same scene.

The creator works in the creator cockpit, one signed-in web page, where
he locks the design, buys paid work and reviews what the framework
reports. Below him, an environment is a table of stages read off its
designs; environment 1's has 261 rows. A \textbf{node} is one stage for
one scene (\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the environment, and one
runner makes, judges and accepts every node. From an approved design
everything runs without a further yes.

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record.
The key hashes the stage and scene, its code version, contracts,
parameters and seed, the pinned model images, every file it reads and
the accepted output of every node it reads. The code version counts only
code that can shape the output; code that decides where and how a job
runs changes what a run costs, never what it makes, and is in no key. An
edit or a fixed fault moves only its own key and those after it, so only
those steps run again. Outputs are kept by content hash, never
overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown,
with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown
blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate
passed and it is no worse than the one accepted before. Later nodes read
only accepted outputs. The creator keeps four actions: reject an
accepted output (every node that read it is then stale), accept a worse
one with a reason, waive one gate for one key, and override the runner
for one paid run. A store that refuses these records from an agent's
process, and a hook, keep agents from them.

\textbf{Batches.} Paid work is bought as a batch with one estimate; the
creator's yes sets the batch's budget across all its tries, and the
money guards stop it there. Inside a batch every paid job runs once; a
failed output becomes a batch gap that the completion gate lists. Every
paid stage is priced from a unit cost measured on the ledgers; a stage
with no measured run has no price, and its estimate is shown apart as a
guess.

\section{Method: building an
environment}\label{method-building-an-environment}

\subsection{From concept to plan}\label{from-concept-to-plan}

\textbf{The environment concept.} The creator first locks the EC in a
short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro {[}31{]}: its style and its physics,
with his reference pictures, and the scenes it holds. An environment
plan places each top scene's site on that ground and works out the sky
each site sees.

\textbf{A concept for each scene, top down.} For each scene a concept is
then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene. Each draw is one
Nano Banana Pro {[}32{]} call, on a concept review page where he marks
areas and asks for a new draw. A child scene is drawn only once its
parent is locked and planned, on a crop of the parent's concept, so it
fits the parent it stands in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} Gemini 3.1 Pro lists every object of the
locked concept as a row of the scene inventory, with its kind, count,
anchor and size in metres, and SAM 3 {[}22{]} boxes each row; nothing is
made or placed that is not a row. Nano Banana Pro then draws the scene
from straight above, and Gemini 3.1 Pro writes the plan in metres from
that drawing, the concept and the inventory: outline, walls, openings,
levels, stairs, roofs, terrain, each item with its facing, people as the
spots they stand on, and the child scenes' rooms. Free checks follow
(every row placed as often as it counts, inside the outline, no two
floor items in each other, every door and standing spot reached on foot,
the child scenes tiling their parent); what remains goes back as notes
at most twice, and only what is left is a gap. The creator's edits on
the plan review page always win and redo what follows.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route is one loop at every scale: a parent
scene gets its concept, plan and a rough model; its plan is split into
the shapes of its child scenes, and once those shapes are fixed every
child scene is made in parallel, each repeating the loop. The rows
\texttt{rough-model} and \texttt{shell-split} are built and have not
run.

\subsection{Layers from row kinds}\label{layers-from-row-kinds}

A scene's layers are read off its design alone: each inventory row's
kind, found from the reading's noun by one table of rules, says what its
objects do (emit, move, lie loose, glow, grow, act, sound). A scene gets
an effects, motion, physics, crops or people layer only for rows that do
so, and light and sound always; each layer is made from the rows by
their kind's rule, so a new environment needs no hand-written list. The
scene record holds what no made piece covers (shells, stairs, ground and
water, backdrop, lamps, neighbours), gated on the shell lying within 5
cm of the plan and every doorway opening onto a floor.

\textbf{Models.} Every object goes the prop route first: Nano Banana Pro
draws its close-up from its crop of the concept (redrawn at most three
times when the shape check rejects it), Pixal3D {[}21{]} builds it, and
a likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with the close-up.
Only an object whose checks fail is built by a code builder a coding
agent writes; a thin straight thing (a pipe, post or beam) whose every
close-up failed is swept along its length in code.

\textbf{Surfaces.} Everything a scene shows comes from the creator's
library, score\_lib, or is a written exception. A model gives shape
only: each part takes one surface from a library of rule-based surfaces,
written as ProcFunc functions {[}33{]} on Infinigen's shaders {[}9{]}
and coloured from the scene's palette. The finish route labels each face
from free renders of the model, matches each finish to a library
surface, bakes it and checks the bake against the close-up (each finish
within ΔE 32 of its colour there). A free library check traces every
material, model, light, sound and character of an accepted assembly to
the library or an exception.

\textbf{Settle and physics.} Loose objects are dropped onto the made
ground; one that would turn more than 15 degrees or drift more than 30
cm is a resting fault and keeps its laid pose. The settle lays where
each object comes to rest as a layer. The physics trial then steps the
composed scene on Newton with MuJoCo's solver on Newton's contacts,
under the gravity of the body the scene stands on, and judges it by
gates: \texttt{reacted}, \texttt{at\ rest}, \texttt{energy} (no rise in
undriven energy) and \texttt{hinge} (a hinged leaf swings to its limit).
The trial moved from Blender's Bullet {[}34{]} to Newton on 27 October
2026. On a toy scene at Moon gravity (two crate drops, a driven door
with a wheel fixed to it, a pushed stool), both engines gave the same
answers on every gate, and Newton landed the crates on their targets
where Bullet left its contact margins of 8 mm; with Newton 1.7.0 in its
deterministic mode, six runs gave bitwise equal bodies in every frame.
The settle moves to Newton the same way, so one engine steps what comes
to rest and what is tried, and one effect is stepped on Newton as a
first case for the effects layer.

\textbf{Light, motion, effects and sound.} The sky is worked out from
the EC's physics, never drawn: each body at its true direction, size and
phase as seen from the scene's site. The lamps are the inventory's
glowing rows; one render per scene gives each lamp its own light group,
and a free sweep mixes their strengths to bring the scene within 1.5
times its concept's brightness. Every object of a moving row moves by
its kind's rule at a speed cited from real machines, gated on nothing
passing within 3 cm of another thing; emitters are measured against real
footage; each sound source gets a library file or one MOSS-SoundEffect
{[}35{]} take, gated on a CLAP {[}36{]} score of at least 0.30.

\textbf{People.} People are models like any other: a close-up per person
row, built by Pixal3D and painted by the finish route. SOMA's body
{[}37{]}, read from the close-up by SAM 3D Body {[}38{]}, is fitted
under it as its rig, and Kimodo's clips {[}39{]} move it; a shell gate
passes a look when 99 percent of its soft points lie within 5 cm of the
body. Each person stands on a person spot of its scene's plan.

\textbf{Sim bodies.} Each character also gets a \textbf{sim body}, its
physical twin on Newton, so people in an environment are simulated, not
only animated. The minimal body is built straight from SOMA's 23
segments with a ball joint at each SOMA joint frame, driven by the
character's SOMA segment rotations with no scaling and no per-person
setup, its mass spread by its segment volumes, standing on a SCORE floor
so its ground forces come from contact; muscles enter as a minimal first
step. Built this way, the body follows SOMA's rotations to within 6.9e-9
m, and on Newton it matches plain MuJoCo to 3.4e-8 after 200 steps.

\textbf{Completion.} A scene is complete when its gate passes on its
final assembly: every row made or dropped with a reason, nothing unmade,
no resting fault, every layer and shown scene present. A fault the
creator ships with is an accepted gap, written with who decided, when
and why; the gate lists every gap by name and is never loosened. A hook
stops an agent claiming a scene is done while its gate fails, since
agents judging their own scenes agree with a measured geometry score
only 45.8 percent of the time {[}18{]}. A scene's films are made from
scripts the creator locked, once it is complete.

\section{Future work: people true to the
person}\label{future-work-people-true-to-the-person}

The minimal sim body is a start. This section states goals, not results.

\textbf{Bodies true to the person.} The sim body is to become fully
musculoskeletal and true to the actual person, older people and a curved
spine included, not a template body resized: joint centres from bone
landmarks on its SOMA mesh, then mass, muscles, joint ranges, spine and
bone shape, each input SOMA lacks marked as assumed with a range. A full
musculoskeletal body with 416 muscles already runs on Newton but still
drifts 1.5 cm from plain MuJoCo after 300 steps, so Newton is to be
validated for bodies before more is built on it. Its joint angles, then
its joint moments and ground forces, are to be held to OpenCap's
LabValidation data.

\textbf{Scenes from motion.} Motion is to come from the SOMA ecosystem:
GEM-X {[}41{]} from video, Kimodo from sentences, and sequences joined
from datasets of both. The terrain a motion implies becomes a SCORE
scene and a sim body follows the motion on it, so the scene keeps the
motion with no video and nothing that identifies a person.

\textbf{Brains.} A \textbf{brain} is a trained model that drives a sim
body's muscles so it moves on its own, including over structures it
never saw. Trained on sim-body data and placed in SCORE's invented
worlds, under their own gravity and ground, it is to adapt to what no
recording holds. Specialised models, for example for clinical groups,
would be trained outside SCORE on data that never enters it.

\textbf{The data loop.} Sim bodies moving in synthesized scenes make
data; that data trains better and more specialised brains; a brain
drives a sim body in new scenes, which makes more data.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}\label{limitations-and-conclusion}

One creator judges, and he has not yet accepted a run. Closed models
(Nano Banana Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro) draw the concepts and close-ups and
read and plan every scene; they may change or go. The design step, the
layers from row kinds, the finish route, the shapes-first loop and the
scene tree have not yet carried a scene through a batch; the edit layer
is a slot, the settle, the effects and the sim body are not yet on
Newton, and no game engine or robot simulator has opened the result yet.
Next: environment 1's batch and its four demonstrations, an engine
adapter, the edit layer carried across assemblies, more environments,
open models in place of the closed ones. In short, SCORE synthesizes an
environment the way studios build one, scene by scene and layer by
layer, in a form a simulator steps as written; an accepted environment
will show whether it holds.
