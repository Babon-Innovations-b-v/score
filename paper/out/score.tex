\begin{abstract}
Games, film, robot learning and embodied AI all depend on 3D
environments that can be simulated, in which every object is a body of
its own with collision, mass and joints, stands under the gravity of the
ground beneath it, can be changed afterwards, and belongs to the person
who made it. Current generative methods rarely deliver this: world
models produce frames that no simulator can step, and one-shot 3D
generators produce a single fused scene without separate bodies or clear
rights to the result. We present SCORE, a framework that synthesises
such environments by building them the way film studios build scenes in
OpenUSD. Each scene sits within its parent scene, and each kind of work
on it (layout, surfaces, physics, light, motion, effects, sound and
people) is a layer of its own that is checked and changed independently
of the others. The creator locks the design from the top down, first an
environment concept that fixes its style and its physics and then a
concept for each scene; open vision foundation models, procedural tools
and coding agents then make every layer below, each checked by measured
gates before the next is made. Every model in the framework allows
commercial use of its output, so the creator remains the author, and
every step is keyed on its inputs, so an edit reruns only the steps that
depend on it. The result is a single OpenUSD environment whose physics a
simulator reads as written; Newton, with MuJoCo's solver, steps it, and
each character in it receives a sim body on Newton driven by its SOMA
segment rotations. We describe the framework and its method, with a
first environment as the evidence; its four demonstrations are marked as
gaps until its batch has run. We close with the route toward simulated
bodies that are true to the individual person and trained models that
drive them. Code and paper are available at
https://github.com/Babon-Innovations-b-v/score (MIT licence).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Simulated 3D environments have become raw material for games, film,
robot learning and embodied AI {[}1{]}. What these uses have in common
is not a particular look but a structure: separate objects with
collision, mass and joints, ground with its own gravity, light and sound
that come from a source in the scene, and a scene that can be opened,
changed and simulated again. Building such an environment by hand is
expensive, and this has made generative methods attractive; yet a
generator that takes over the work must leave the result in its maker's
hands, and many of those makers are sceptical, with 52 percent of game
workers in GDC's 2026 survey reporting that they thought generative AI
was hurting their industry {[}2{]}.

The generative methods available today fall short of that structure in
different ways. World models generate an environment as a sequence of
frames {[}3{]}, {[}4{]}, which no simulator can step. 3D environment
generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} produce geometry, but mostly as one fused
asset from which separate objects have to be recovered afterwards
{[}7{]}, {[}8{]}. Both work in a single shot, so a fault in the result
cannot be traced back to the step that caused it. Procedural systems
{[}9{]} and coding agents that write scenes as programs {[}10{]},
{[}11{]} keep the environment explicit, but they rebuild scenes that
already exist or draw from a fixed library, and their objects often
float or come apart {[}12{]}. Open vision foundation models {[}13{]} can
now read shapes {[}14{]}, masks {[}15{]} and depth {[}16{]} from
pictures, and a coding agent can connect them as tools, but none of them
keeps one style across objects that different models made {[}17{]},
{[}18{]}, and none of them provides physics.

The pieces needed for the physics do exist. OpenUSD {[}19{]}, the scene
description Pixar developed for its films, keeps each department's work
in a layer of its own and assembles scenes from smaller ones by
reference, and its physics schema states bodies, colliders, mass and
joints within the same stage. MuJoCo {[}20{]} is a widely used solver
for articulated bodies in contact, and Newton {[}21{]}, built on Warp
{[}22{]}, runs MuJoCo's solver on the GPU {[}23{]} and reads OpenUSD
directly. What is missing is a way to get from what a creator wants to a
complete environment in that form.

We observe that an environment need not be generated in one shot to be
generated automatically. Studios already build scenes as a tree of
smaller scenes, each a stack of layers that one department makes and
another can change without touching the rest, and the same structure
lets a machine build an environment one checked step at a time. If every
layer is made from a design the creator has locked, by a step whose
output is measured before anything is built on it, then a fault has an
address, an edit has a bounded cost, and the physics can be written into
the record itself rather than recovered from it.

This paper contributes not a new generative model but a framework that
turns a creator's locked design into one OpenUSD environment which a
simulator steps as written. What is new is the combination of three
elements: a design locked from the top down, from an environment concept
with its style and physics to a concept for every scene, from which
every layer is read; layers made by keyed steps, each held to measured
gates, so that an edit reruns only what it changes; and a record whose
bodies, joints and gravity are written in OpenUSD's physics schema, made
only with models that allow commercial use of their output. The
environments this yields are what the name SCORE describes. They are
complete, in that every object, surface, light and sound a scene shows
exists wherever the viewer looks; owned, because every model in the
framework allows commercial use of its output, so the creator holds the
full rights; and responsive, because every object has its collider, mass
and material and what moves has its joints. They are also editable,
since each scene is a stack of layers and each layer is made by a keyed
step. An environment can be as small as a house on a plot of ground or
as large as a system of worlds that move one another.

Section 2 describes the framework, Section 3 its method, the stages that
build a scene from the top down and layer by layer, and Section 4 the
evidence, four demonstrations on a first environment that remain gaps
until its batch has run. Section 5 discusses what the design implies and
where it is limited, Section 6 positions it against related work, and
Section 7 sets out where the framework goes next, toward simulated
people who are true to the individual person.

\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

An environment in SCORE is one OpenUSD record. Each scene is one stage,
which sits within its parent scene and holds its child scenes, each
referenced at the room its parent's plan gives it, so that opening a top
scene opens everything below it. Within a scene the layers stack in a
fixed order: a slot for the creator's edit layer on top, then people,
sound, light, motion, effects, crops, machines, physics, settle and
ground, and at the bottom the base laid from the locked plan. Each
object corresponds to one real-world object and carries its kind, its
transform, its collider and a library surface for each of its parts.

The physics of an environment is decided in its environment concept
(EC). It may be a plot of ground with a gravity and a path of the sun,
one world, or a system of bodies that move one another; a real body
takes its values from a cited table, and an invented one is given by its
radius, mass, orbit and spin. The physics layer writes each scene's
bodies, masses, joints and the gravity of the body it stands on in
OpenUSD's physics schema, so that any reader of that schema obtains the
same scene.

The creator works in the creator cockpit, a single signed-in web page in
which he locks the design, buys paid work and reviews what the framework
reports. Beneath that page, an environment is a table of stages read off
its designs; for environment 1 the table has 261 rows. A node is one
stage applied to one scene (for example \texttt{assembly@hangar}) or to
the environment as a whole, and a single runner makes, judges and
accepts every node. Once a design is approved, everything runs without a
further yes from the creator.

Three mechanisms make this safe to leave running. The first is the key.
A node is made only when its key has no finish record, and the key
hashes the stage and scene, its code version, contracts, parameters and
seed, the pinned model images, every file the node reads and the
accepted output of every node it reads. The code version counts only
code that can shape the output; code that decides where and how a job
runs changes what a run costs but never what it makes, and is therefore
kept out of the key. An edit or a fixed fault moves only its own key and
those downstream of it, so only those steps run again, and outputs are
kept by content hash and never overwritten.

The second is the gate. Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown,
together with its measures and a reason; missing evidence counts as
unknown, and unknown blocks a node just as fail does. The runner accepts
an output by itself when every gate has passed and the output is no
worse than the one accepted before it, and later nodes read only
accepted outputs. The creator keeps four actions for himself: he may
reject an accepted output, which makes every node that read it stale;
accept a worse one with a reason; waive one gate for one key; and
override the runner for one paid run. A store that refuses these records
when they come from an agent's process, together with a hook, keeps
agents from taking these actions.

The third is the batch. Paid work is bought as a batch with a single
estimate, and the creator's approval sets one budget for the batch
across all its tries, at which the money guards stop it. Within a batch
every paid job runs once, and an output that fails becomes a batch gap
that the completion gate lists. Every paid stage is priced from a unit
cost measured on the ledgers; a stage that has never run has no measured
price, so its estimate is shown separately as a guess.

\section{Method: building an
environment}\label{method-building-an-environment}

The method has two parts. The first turns what the creator wants into a
locked design: a concept for the environment and for each of its scenes,
an inventory of every object, and a plan in metres. The second reads the
layers each scene needs off that design and makes each in turn, behind
its gates, until the scene passes its completion gate.

\subsection{From concept to plan}\label{from-concept-to-plan}

Step 1: the environment concept. The creator first locks the EC in a
short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro {[}24{]}, in which he fixes its
style and its physics, adds his reference pictures and names the scenes
it holds. An environment plan then places each top scene's site on that
ground and works out the sky each site sees.

Step 2: a concept for each scene. A concept is then locked for every
scene, in the order of parent scene before child scene. Each draw is one
Nano Banana Pro {[}25{]} call, made on a concept review page where the
creator can mark areas and ask for a new draw. A child scene is drawn
only once its parent has been locked and planned, and it is drawn on a
crop of the parent's concept, so that it fits the parent it stands in.

Step 3: inventory and plan. Gemini 3.1 Pro lists every object of the
locked concept as a row of the scene inventory, giving its kind, count,
anchor and size in metres, and SAM 3 {[}15{]} draws a box around each
row; nothing that is not a row is made or placed. Nano Banana Pro then
draws the scene from straight above, and Gemini 3.1 Pro writes the plan
in metres from that drawing, the concept and the inventory. The plan
gives the outline, walls, openings, levels, stairs, roofs and terrain,
each item with its facing, people as the spots on which they stand, and
the rooms of the child scenes. A set of free checks follows: every row
must be placed as often as its count says, lie inside the outline and
not overlap another floor item, every door and standing spot must be
reachable on foot, and the child scenes must tile their parent. What
fails is returned to the model as notes at most twice, and only what is
left after that becomes a gap. The creator's own edits on the plan
review page always take precedence and redo what follows from them.

The route is the same loop at every scale, so that shapes are fixed
before detail. A parent scene receives its concept, its plan and a rough
model; its plan is then split into the shapes of its child scenes, and
once those shapes are fixed every child scene is made in parallel, each
repeating the loop. The two stages that carry this, \texttt{rough-model}
and \texttt{shell-split}, are built but have not yet run.

\subsection{Layers from row kinds}\label{layers-from-row-kinds}

A scene's layers are read off its design alone. Each inventory row's
kind, found from the noun the reading gave it by a single table of
rules, says what its objects do: emit, move, lie loose, glow, grow, act
or sound. A scene receives an effects, motion, physics, crops or people
layer only when it has rows that do so, and always receives light and
sound. Each layer is made from its rows by the rule for their kind, so a
new environment needs no hand-written list of what to build. The scene
record holds what no made object covers (shells, stairs, ground and
water, backdrop, lamps and neighbouring buildings), and it is gated on
the shell lying within 5 cm of the plan and on every doorway opening
onto a floor.

Models are made by the prop route first. Nano Banana Pro draws a
close-up of each object from its crop of the concept, redrawn at most
three times when the shape check rejects it; Pixal3D {[}14{]} builds the
model from that close-up; and a likeness gate compares the model's
outline from eight sides with the close-up. Only an object whose checks
fail is built instead by a code builder that a coding agent writes, and
a thin straight object (a pipe, post or beam) whose every close-up has
failed is swept along its length in code.

Surfaces come from the creator's library. Everything a scene shows is
drawn from score\_lib or recorded as a written exception, and a model
contributes its shape only. Each of its parts takes one surface from a
library of rule-based surfaces, written as ProcFunc functions {[}26{]}
on Infinigen's shaders {[}9{]} and coloured from the scene's palette.
The finish route labels each face from free renders of the model,
matches each finish to a library surface, bakes it, and checks the bake
against the close-up, requiring each finish to lie within ΔE 32 of its
colour there. A free library check then traces every material, model,
light, sound and character of an accepted assembly back to the library
or to an exception.

\subsection{Settling and physics}\label{settling-and-physics}

Loose objects are dropped onto the made ground, and the settle writes
where each comes to rest as a layer of its own; an object that would
turn by more than 15 degrees or drift by more than 30 cm is recorded as
a resting fault and keeps its laid pose. The physics trial then steps
the composed scene on Newton, using MuJoCo's solver on Newton's contacts
under the gravity of the body the scene stands on, and judges it by four
gates: \texttt{reacted}, \texttt{at\ rest}, \texttt{energy}, which
requires that undriven energy does not rise, and \texttt{hinge}, which
requires a hinged leaf to swing to its limit.

The trial moved from Blender's Bullet {[}27{]} to Newton on 27 October
2026, and the move was checked on a toy scene at Moon gravity with two
crate drops, a driven door with a wheel fixed to it and a pushed stool.
Both engines gave the same answer on every gate, and Newton landed the
crates on their targets where Bullet left them on its 8 mm contact
margins. Newton's deterministic mode was bitwise equal over six runs on
that toy, but it cannot hold scenes with many loose bodies, because its
buffers then pass the 32-bit limit; the trial and the settle therefore
run without it, and two runs of a 25-body toy agree within 5e-7 m on
every body except one that falls off the edge of the floor. The settle
is moving to Newton in the same way, so that one engine steps both what
comes to rest and what is tried, and one effect is to be stepped on
Newton as a first case for the effects layer.

\subsection{Light, motion, effects and
sound}\label{light-motion-effects-and-sound}

The sky is computed from the EC's physics rather than drawn, with each
body placed at its true direction, size and phase as seen from the
scene's site. The lamps are the inventory's glowing rows: one render per
scene gives each lamp its own light group, and a free sweep mixes their
strengths until the scene lies within 1.5 times its concept's
brightness. Every object of a moving row moves by the rule for its kind,
at a speed cited from real machines, and is gated on nothing passing
within 3 cm of anything else. Emitters are measured against real
footage, and each sound source receives either a library file or one
MOSS-SoundEffect {[}28{]} take, gated on a CLAP {[}29{]} score of at
least 0.30.

\subsection{People and sim bodies}\label{people-and-sim-bodies}

People are made like any other model, from a close-up per person row
that Pixal3D builds and the finish route paints. SOMA's body {[}30{]},
read from the close-up by SAM 3D Body {[}31{]}, is fitted beneath the
model as its rig, and Kimodo's clips {[}32{]} move it; a shell gate
passes a look when 99 percent of its soft points lie within 5 cm of the
body. Each person stands on a person spot of its scene's plan.

Each character also receives a sim body, its physical twin on Newton, so
that the people in an environment are simulated and not only animated.
The minimal body is built directly from SOMA's 23 segments, with a ball
joint at each SOMA joint frame, and is driven by the character's SOMA
segment rotations without scaling or per-person setup. Its mass is
distributed by segment volume, and it stands on a SCORE floor, so its
ground forces come from contact; muscles enter as a minimal first step.
Built in this way, the body follows SOMA's rotations to within 6.9e-9 m,
and on Newton it matches plain MuJoCo to within 3.4e-8 after 200 steps.

\subsection{Completion}\label{completion}

A scene is complete when its completion gate passes on its final
assembly, which requires every row to be made or dropped with a reason,
nothing to be left unmade, no resting fault, and every layer and every
shown scene to be present. A fault the creator decides to ship with
becomes an accepted gap, recorded with who decided, when and why; the
gate lists every gap by name and is never loosened. A hook stops an
agent from claiming that a scene is done while its gate fails, because
agents judging their own scenes agree with a measured geometry score
only 45.8 percent of the time {[}34{]}. Once a scene is complete, its
films are made from scripts the creator has locked.

The division of labour behind this work is as follows. The creator's
locks, picks, accepts and waivers are his own clicks, and everything
between them runs automatically. Coding agents wrote most of the
framework's code under the author's direction: 2,413 of its 2,436
commits carry a coding agent as co-author. Hand fixes to a scene are
recorded as the creator's edits.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The design rests on one observation, that an environment generated step
by step, with every step held to a measured gate and keyed on its
inputs, can carry its physics and its authorship in the record itself.
The consequences of this are concrete, although until environment 1's
batch has run they are properties of the design and not yet measured
results:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Faults have an address.} Because each layer is made by its own
  node and judged by its own gates, a fault is found at the step that
  caused it and not in a finished scene, and fixing it moves only the
  keys downstream of that step. Demonstration 4 is to measure what this
  saves.
\item
  \textbf{One record for many readers.} The physics is written in
  OpenUSD's physics schema and not in one engine's format, so any reader
  of that schema obtains the same bodies, joints and gravity.
  Demonstration 3 is to test this in a game engine, a robot simulator
  and a film renderer.
\item
  \textbf{The creator stays the author.} Every model allows commercial
  use of its output, every finish traces to the creator's library, and
  the decisions that shape the result (locks, accepts, rejects and
  waivers) are his own, recorded with their reasons.
\item
  \textbf{Agents are not their own judges.} A coding agent writes code
  builders and connects tools, but it never decides that its own work is
  done; that decision belongs to measured gates and, above them, to the
  creator.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

\textbf{One creator and no accepted run.} A single creator judges every
environment, and he has not yet accepted a run; the method has therefore
not yet been shown to produce an environment its maker would ship.

\textbf{Closed models.} Nano Banana Pro and Gemini 3.1 Pro draw the
concepts and close-ups and read and plan every scene. They are closed
and may change or be withdrawn, and replacing them with open models
remains to be done.

\textbf{Unexercised stages.} The design step, the layers from row kinds,
the finish route, the shapes-first loop and the scene tree have not yet
carried a scene through a batch. The edit layer is still a slot, the
settle, the effects and the sim body are not yet on Newton, and no game
engine or robot simulator has yet opened the result.

\section{Related work}\label{related-work}

SCORE sits between three lines of work. World models {[}3{]}, {[}4{]}
and 3D environment generators {[}5{]}, {[}6{]} generate an environment
in one shot, as frames or as a fused asset, and recent work recovers
separate objects from that asset afterwards {[}7{]}, {[}8{]}; SCORE
instead makes every object separately from the start, and writes its
body into the record. Procedural systems {[}9{]}, {[}35{]} keep scenes
explicit through hand-written generators, and SCORE uses their shaders,
through ProcFunc {[}26{]}, as the base of its surface library, while
reading what to build from a creator's concept rather than from a fixed
grammar. Coding agents that write scenes as programs {[}10{]}, {[}11{]},
{[}36{]}, {[}37{]}, {[}38{]}, {[}39{]} are closest in spirit, but the
scenes they write often float or come apart {[}12{]}, they judge their
own geometry unreliably {[}34{]}, and on scenes rebuilt from video the
strongest model scores 0.91 on static scenes but only 0.67 on dynamic
ones {[}40{]}. SCORE therefore gives such agents a narrow role, writing
builders for the objects other routes fail on, and leaves judgement to
measured gates.

\section{Future work: people true to the
person}\label{future-work-people-true-to-the-person}

The minimal sim body is a starting point, and this section states goals
rather than results.

The sim body is to become fully musculoskeletal and true to the
individual person, older people and those with a curved spine included,
rather than a template body resized to fit. Its joint centres are to
come from bone landmarks on its SOMA mesh, followed by its mass,
muscles, joint ranges, spine and bone shape, with each input that SOMA
lacks marked as assumed and given a range. A full musculoskeletal body
with 416 muscles already runs on Newton, but it still drifts 1.5 cm from
plain MuJoCo after 300 steps, so Newton is to be validated for bodies
before more is built on it. The body's joint angles, and then its joint
moments and ground forces, are to be held to OpenCap's LabValidation
data.

Motion is to come from the SOMA ecosystem: from video through GEM-X
{[}41{]}, from sentences through Kimodo, and from sequences joined
across datasets of both. The terrain a motion implies then becomes a
SCORE scene on which a sim body follows the motion, so that the scene
keeps the motion without the video and without anything that identifies
a person.

Beyond following recorded motion, we intend to train a brain, a model
that drives a sim body's muscles so that it moves on its own, including
over structures it has never seen. Trained on sim-body data and placed
in SCORE's invented worlds, under their own gravity and ground, a brain
is to adapt to situations no recording holds. Specialised models, for
example for clinical groups, would be trained outside SCORE on data that
never enters it. Together these form a data loop: sim bodies moving in
synthesised scenes produce data, that data trains better and more
specialised brains, and a brain driving a sim body in new scenes
produces more data.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Simulable environments have been expensive to make because each object,
surface, light, sound and body had to be built and joined by hand, and
the generative methods that promise to remove that cost produce frames
or fused geometry that a simulator cannot step and a creator cannot
fully own. SCORE takes a different route. It builds an environment the
way studios build a scene, scene within scene and layer by layer, from a
design the creator has locked, with every layer made by a keyed step and
held to measured gates, and it writes the result as one OpenUSD record
whose physics a simulator steps as written. The next steps are
environment 1's batch and its four demonstrations, an engine adapter,
the edit layer carried across assemblies, further environments, and open
models in place of the closed ones. If an accepted environment confirms
that the approach holds, we hope it brings owned, simulable worlds
within reach of individual creators, researchers and small teams who
could not afford to build them by hand, and gives simulated people a
place to move in.
