\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Simulated 3D environments have become raw material for games, film, robot learning and embodied AI \citep{yang2024holodeck}. What these uses have in common is not a particular look but a structure: separate objects with collision, mass and joints, ground with its own gravity, light and sound that come from a source in the scene, and a scene that can be opened, changed and simulated again. Building such an environment by hand is expensive, and this has made generative methods attractive; yet a generator that takes over the work must leave the result in its maker's hands, and many of those makers are sceptical, with 52 percent of game workers in GDC's 2026 survey reporting that they thought generative AI was hurting their industry \citep{gdc2026survey}.

The generative methods available today fall short of that structure in different ways. World models generate an environment as a sequence of frames \citep{bruce2024genie, genie3}, which no simulator can step. 3D environment generators \citep{wang2025worldgen, hyworld2} produce geometry, but mostly as one fused asset from which separate objects have to be recovered afterwards \citep{hu2026worldact, niu2026worldsculpt}. Both work in a single shot, so a fault in the result cannot be traced back to the step that caused it. Procedural systems \citep{raistrick2023infinigen} and coding agents that write scenes as programs \citep{hu2024scenecraft, wang2026codeasworlds} keep the environment explicit, but they rebuild scenes that already exist or draw from a fixed library, and their objects often float or come apart \citep{gao2026codebench3d}. Open vision foundation models \citep{oquab2023dinov2} can now read shapes \citep{li2026pixal3d}, masks \citep{carion2025sam3} and depth \citep{wang2025moge2} from pictures, and a coding agent can connect them as tools, but none of them keeps one style across objects that different models made \citep{wu2026production, yang2026flowscene}, and none of them provides physics.

The pieces needed for the physics do exist. OpenUSD \citep{openusd}, the scene description Pixar developed for its films, keeps each department's work in a layer of its own and assembles scenes from smaller ones by reference, and its physics schema states bodies, colliders, mass and joints within the same stage. MuJoCo \citep{todorov2012mujoco} is a widely used solver for articulated bodies in contact, and Newton \citep{newton}, built on Warp \citep{warp}, runs MuJoCo's solver on the GPU \citep{mujocowarp} and reads OpenUSD directly. What is missing is a way to get from what a creator wants to a complete environment in that form.

We observe that an environment need not be generated in one shot to be generated automatically. Studios already build scenes as a tree of smaller scenes, each a stack of layers that one department makes and another can change without touching the rest, and the same structure lets a machine build an environment one checked step at a time. If every layer is made from a design the creator has locked, by a step whose output is measured before anything is built on it, then a fault has an address, an edit has a bounded cost, and the physics can be written into the record itself rather than recovered from it.

This paper contributes not a new generative model but a framework that turns a creator's locked design into one OpenUSD environment which a simulator steps as written. What is new is the combination of three elements: a design locked from the top down, from an environment concept with its style and physics to a concept for every scene, from which every layer is read; layers made by keyed steps, each held to measured gates, so that an edit reruns only what it changes; and a record whose bodies, joints and gravity are written in OpenUSD's physics schema, made only with models that allow commercial use of their output. The environments this yields are what the name SCORE describes. They are complete, in that every object, surface, light and sound a scene shows exists wherever the viewer looks; owned, because every model in the framework allows commercial use of its output, so the creator holds the full rights; and responsive, because every object has its collider, mass and material and what moves has its joints. They are also editable, since each scene is a stack of layers and each layer is made by a keyed step. An environment can be as small as a house on a plot of ground or as large as a system of worlds that move one another.

Section 2 describes the framework, Section 3 its method, the stages that build a scene from the top down and layer by layer, and Section 4 the evidence, four demonstrations on a first environment that remain gaps until its batch has run. Section 5 discusses what the design implies and where it is limited, Section 6 positions it against related work, and Section 7 sets out where the framework goes next, toward simulated people who are true to the individual person.
