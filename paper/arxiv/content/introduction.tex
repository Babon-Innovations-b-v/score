\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Interactive 3D environments are the raw material of games, film and embodied AI \citep{yang2024holodeck}, and building one is expensive: every object is modelled, surfaced, placed, given collision, lit and given sound in one style, and the environment stays editable. Generative models promise to take over much of this, but in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game workers thought generative AI was hurting the industry \citep{gdc2026survey}; a tool for building environments has to leave the result in the creator's hands.

Studios already have a standard for this: OpenUSD \citep{openusd}, the scene description Pixar made for its films. Each artist works in a file of their own, a layer; the layers are stacked in a stated order, so departments work on one scene without editing each other's work, and scenes are assembled from smaller ones by reference. In this convention a scene sits within its parent scene and holds its child scenes, and each department's work on it (layout, set dressing, surfaces, light, effects, motion, sound) is a layer that can be checked and changed apart from the rest. SCORE builds environments by this convention, with the creator deciding and the framework doing the departments' work. The creator locks an environment concept (EC): its style and its physics. The EC holds the scenes and their layering. For each scene a concept is then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene, and from it the framework makes every layer below, each its own OpenUSD layer, checked before the next is made.

World models generate an environment as frames \citep{bruce2024genie, genie3} that no engine can open. 3D environment generators \citep{wang2025worldgen, hyworld2} produce geometry, but mostly as one fused asset, from which separate objects are recovered after the fact \citep{hu2026worldact, niu2026worldsculpt}. Both work in one shot, so a creator cannot find or change the step behind a fault. Procedural systems \citep{raistrick2023infinigen, raistrick2024indoors} and coding agents that write scenes as programs \citep{hu2024scenecraft, li2026rcwm, guo2026worldclaw, yin2026autoue, he2026thinking, wang2026codeasworlds} keep the environment explicit, but rebuild a scene that already exists or draw their objects from a fixed library; their models often float or come apart \citep{gao2026codebench3d}, and such agents judge their own geometry unreliably \citep{li2026lego}; on scenes rebuilt from video, the best model scores 0.91 on static scenes but 0.67 on dynamic ones \citep{shen2026codebench4d}, which is why SCORE checks motion as a layer of its own. Open vision foundation models \citep{oquab2023dinov2} now read shapes \citep{li2026pixal3d}, masks \citep{carion2025sam3}, depth \citep{wang2025moge2} and parts \citep{lin2025partcrafter} out of pictures, and a coding agent can connect them as tools; none of them provides one style across objects that different models made, which the field names as an open problem \citep{wu2026production, yang2026flowscene}.

SCORE's environments are meant to be \textbf{complete}: every object, surface, light and sound exists wherever the viewer looks. They are \textbf{owned}: every model in the framework allows commercial use of its output, so the creator stays the author, holding the full commercial rights. They are meant to be \textbf{responsive}: every object has real collision, mass and material. And they can be any kind of \textbf{environment}, from a house on a plot of ground to a system of worlds. To our knowledge, no other openly available environment generation work joins vision foundation models into engine-ready environments that are built from reference pictures, steered by a person's choices at each step, and made only with models that allow commercial use of their output.

We contribute \textbf{the SCORE framework}, which builds an environment by the studios' convention, the creator deciding top down and the framework making every layer below (Section 2); \textbf{measured gates} for every layer of a scene and a completion gate over them (Section 3); and \textbf{evidence from a first environment}, left as gaps until its remake has run (Section 4).
