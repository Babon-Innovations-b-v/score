\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Simulated 3D environments are raw material for games, film, robot learning and embodied AI \citep{yang2024holodeck}. What these uses share is not a look but a structure: separate objects with collision, mass and joints, ground with a gravity, light and sound that come from somewhere, and a scene that can be opened, changed and simulated again. Building one by hand is expensive, and a generator that takes the work over has to leave the result in its maker's hands: in GDC's 2026 survey 52 percent of game workers thought generative AI was hurting the industry \citep{gdc2026survey}.

World models generate an environment as frames \citep{bruce2024genie, genie3} that no simulator can step. 3D environment generators \citep{wang2025worldgen, hyworld2} produce geometry, mostly as one fused asset from which separate objects are recovered after the fact \citep{hu2026worldact, niu2026worldsculpt}. Both work in one shot, so a fault cannot be traced to the step behind it. Procedural systems \citep{raistrick2023infinigen, raistrick2024indoors} and coding agents that write scenes as programs \citep{hu2024scenecraft, li2026rcwm, guo2026worldclaw, yin2026autoue, he2026thinking, wang2026codeasworlds} keep the environment explicit, but rebuild a scene that already exists or draw from a fixed library; their models often float or come apart \citep{gao2026codebench3d}, such agents judge their own geometry unreliably \citep{li2026lego}, and on scenes rebuilt from video the best model scores 0.91 on static scenes but 0.67 on dynamic ones \citep{shen2026codebench4d}. Open vision foundation models \citep{oquab2023dinov2} now read shapes \citep{li2026pixal3d}, masks \citep{carion2025sam3} and depth \citep{wang2025moge2} out of pictures, and a coding agent can connect them as tools; none gives one style across objects that different models made \citep{wu2026production, yang2026flowscene}, and none gives physics.

The pieces for the physics exist. OpenUSD \citep{openusd}, the scene description Pixar made for its films, layers each department's work in a file of its own and assembles scenes from smaller ones by reference, and its physics schema states bodies, colliders, mass and joints in the same stage. MuJoCo \citep{todorov2012mujoco} is a widely used solver for articulated bodies with contact; Newton \citep{newton}, built on Warp \citep{warp}, runs MuJoCo's solver on the GPU \citep{mujocowarp} and reads OpenUSD directly. What is missing is a way to get from what a creator wants to a complete environment in that form.

SCORE is that framework. Its environments are \textbf{complete}: every object, surface, light and sound a scene shows exists wherever the viewer looks. They are \textbf{owned}: every model in the framework allows commercial use of its output, so the creator holds the full rights. They are \textbf{responsive}: every object has its collider, mass and material, and what moves has its joints, so a simulator steps the environment as written. And they are \textbf{editable}: each scene is a stack of layers, each layer made by a keyed step, so a change runs again only what it touches. An environment can be a house on a plot of ground or a system of worlds that move one another.

We contribute \textbf{the SCORE framework} (Section 2), which turns a creator's locked design into one OpenUSD environment that is physically simulable and opens unchanged in a game engine, a robot simulator or a film renderer; \textbf{its method} (Section 3), the stages that build a scene top down and layer by layer, each behind measured gates; and \textbf{the evidence plan} (Section 4), four demonstrations on a first environment, left as gaps until its batch has run. Section 5 sets out where the framework goes next, toward simulated people true to the person.
