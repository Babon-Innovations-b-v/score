\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Simulated 3D environments serve games, film, robot learning and embodied AI \citep{yang2024holodeck}. Across these uses they share a common structure of separate objects with collision geometry, mass and joints, a ground surface with its gravity, light and sound emitted by identifiable sources, and scenes that can be reopened, modified and simulated again. Authoring such an environment by hand is expensive, and a generative system that takes over this work must leave the result in the hands of its author, since in the Game Developers Conference's 2026 survey 52 percent of game workers considered generative AI to be harming the industry \citep{gdc2026survey}.

World models generate an environment as a sequence of video frames \citep{bruce2024genie, genie3} that no physics simulator can step forward in time. 3D environment generators \citep{wang2025worldgen, hyworld2} produce geometry, mostly as a single fused asset from which separate objects have to be recovered afterwards \citep{hu2026worldact, niu2026worldsculpt}. Both operate in a single pass, so a fault in the output cannot be traced to the step that caused it. Procedural content generation systems \citep{raistrick2023infinigen, raistrick2024indoors} and coding agents, language models that write scenes as executable programs \citep{hu2024scenecraft, li2026rcwm, guo2026worldclaw, yin2026autoue, he2026thinking, wang2026codeasworlds}, keep the environment explicit and inspectable, but they either reconstruct a scene that already exists or assemble it from a fixed asset library. The models they produce often float above their supports or come apart \citep{gao2026codebench3d}, such agents assess their own geometry unreliably \citep{li2026lego}, and on scenes reconstructed from video the strongest model scores 0.91 on static scenes but only 0.67 on dynamic ones \citep{shen2026codebench4d}. Open vision foundation models \citep{oquab2023dinov2}, large models pretrained on broad image data and reused across tasks, now recover 3D shape \citep{li2026pixal3d}, segmentation masks \citep{carion2025sam3} and depth \citep{wang2025moge2} from images, and a coding agent can call them as tools, yet none of them gives a consistent visual style across objects that different models produced \citep{wu2026production, yang2026flowscene}, and none gives physical properties.

The components needed for the physics already exist. OpenUSD \citep{openusd}, the scene description that Pixar developed for its film production, stores each department's work in its own layer file and composes scenes from smaller ones by reference, and its physics schema describes rigid bodies, colliders, mass and joints within the same stage, the composed scene. MuJoCo \citep{todorov2012mujoco} is a widely used physics engine for articulated bodies in contact, and Newton \citep{newton}, built on the Warp GPU programming framework \citep{warp}, runs MuJoCo's solver on the GPU \citep{mujocowarp} and loads OpenUSD scenes directly. What is missing is a route from what a creator intends to a complete environment in this form.

SCORE is that framework. Its environments are complete, since every object, surface, light and sound a scene shows exists wherever the viewer looks. They are owned, because every model in the framework permits commercial use of its output, so the creator holds the full rights. They are responsive, because every object carries its collider, mass and material and every moving part its joints, so a simulator steps the environment as written. They are editable, since each scene is a stack of layers produced by steps that are each identified by a key derived from their inputs, and a change reruns only the steps it affects. An environment can range from a house on a plot of ground to a system of worlds that move one another by gravitation.

We contribute the SCORE framework (Section 2), which turns a creator's locked design into a single OpenUSD environment that is physically simulable and opens unchanged in a game engine, a robot simulator or a film renderer. Its method (Section 3) builds a scene from the top down and layer by layer, with each stage passing measured acceptance tests before the next begins. The evidence plan (Section 4) comprises four demonstrations on a first environment, left as gaps until its batch has run. Section 5 sets out where the framework goes next, towards simulated people whose bodies are true to the individual person.
