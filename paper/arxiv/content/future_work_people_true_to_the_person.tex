\section{Future work: people true to the person}\label{future-work-people-true-to-the-person}

The current sim body is a starting point. This section describes goals, not results.

\textbf{Bodies true to the person.} The sim body is to become a musculoskeletal model of the individual person rather than a scaled generic template, including older people and people with a curved spine. MyoSuite's MyoFullBody \citep{caggiano2022myosuite}, a full-body model with 416 muscles whose arm and hand derive from the MoBL-ARMS model \citep{holzbaur2005upper, saul2015benchmarking}, is the starting point. Each segment of the SOMA body is fitted to the group of bones that spans the same part of the body, from the same joint to the same joint, so that the joints within a group, such as the vertebrae of the spine, share its rotation within their ranges of motion. Virtual markers on bony landmarks of the SOMA mesh add the shape of this person's skeleton, and segment masses are derived from the volumes of the SOMA body. Every input that SOMA does not provide is marked as assumed and given a plausible range. MyoFullBody already runs in Newton, but it still drifts 1.5 cm from plain MuJoCo after 300 steps, so Newton must first be validated for such bodies. The joint angles, and then the net joint moments and ground reaction forces, are to be validated against OpenCap's LabValidation data.

\textbf{Scenes from motion.} Motion comes from the SOMA ecosystem: estimated from video with GEM-X \citep{gemx}, or generated from text and constraints with Kimodo \citep{rempe2026kimodo}, in practically unlimited quantity. The terrain a movement implies becomes a SCORE scene, so the movement keeps its setting without the source video or anything that identifies a person. The sim body follows each movement on the scene's own ground. For every frame, the simulation solves for the ground reaction forces from contact, the net joint moments, and the activations and forces of the muscles that reproduce the movement. Whatever force and moment neither the ground nor the muscles can supply remains at the pelvis as the residual. The residual is recorded and never applied to the body, so it measures how dynamically consistent the movement is, and only movements with a small residual are kept. Biomechanics uses the same residual to assess recorded motion \citep{delp2007opensim, werling2023addbiomechanics}, and PhysibleMDM \citep{stolzle2026physiblemdm} minimises an inverse-dynamics residual to make generated motion feasible for a torque-driven robot. We apply the criterion to generated motion in a muscle-driven body.

\textbf{Layered training sets.} The same simulations yield training sets in layers. Each sample pairs a rendered image of a clothed person in a complete scene with ground truth for every layer beneath it: the SOMA body and its joint angles, the ground reaction force under each foot, the muscle activations and forces, the residual, and the depth, segmentation and layout of the scene. Their main use is to train estimators that infer muscle activations, joint moments and ground reaction forces directly from ordinary video, fast enough for routine clinical use. Recorded data cannot supply this at scale: muscle forces cannot be measured without invasive methods, and force plates and electromyography confine measurement to a laboratory. Simulation can generate the bodies, movements and settings an estimator must cover, including those of a specific patient group. BioHuman10M \citep{huo2026biohuman} already pairs video, part of it rendered, with the activations of a 112-muscle model and with ground reaction forces predicted by a learned model. Our sets would add scenes generated for the purpose under varied lighting, terrain and clothing, ground reaction forces from contact with each scene's own ground, a 416-muscle model, and a residual for every sample. Their value depends on whether estimators trained on them transfer to real force-plate and electromyography recordings.

\textbf{Brains.} A brain is a trained controller that drives a sim body's muscles. Trained on the retained movements and the muscle forces that produce them, it is placed in SCORE's synthesised worlds under their own gravity and terrain, where it must adapt to situations no recording contains. Every run it drives adds movement to the training sets, so it extends the data beyond what a motion generator produces. Muscle-driven controllers such as KINESIS \citep{simos2025kinesis} and MuscleMimic \citep{li2026musclemimic} are trained on motion capture, as are torque-driven ones such as PHC \citep{luo2023phc}. To our knowledge, no muscle-driven controller has yet been trained on generated motion selected by its residual. That claim holds only once such a controller is compared with one trained on motion capture and with one trained on the same generated motion unfiltered.

\textbf{The data loop.} The amount of data scales with the compute put behind it, since every scene, movement and run adds samples. This is why SCORE is open: others can apply their own compute to the same framework and share the resulting datasets. Estimators specialised for clinical use would be trained outside SCORE, on data that never enters it.
