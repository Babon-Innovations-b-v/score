\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

The creator works in the creator cockpit, one signed-in web page: there he decides the design, buys paid work and reviews what the framework reports. Below him, an environment is a table of stages read off its designs; environment 1's has 261 rows today, 201 of them the bodies of the 67 people its designs show. A \textbf{node} is one stage for one scene (\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the environment, and one runner makes, judges and accepts every node. From an approved design everything runs without a further yes.

\subsection{Keys, gates and accepts}\label{keys-gates-and-accepts}

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record. The key hashes the stage and scene, its code version, contracts, parameters and seed, the pinned model images, every file it reads and the accepted output of every node it reads. The code version counts the code that can shape the output: the stage's maker, the scripts it sends to a machine and every module they import; code that only decides where and how a job runs (renting, the pool of warm machines, the ledger, money guards, the cockpit) changes what a run costs, never what it makes, and is in no key. A fault fixed in one stage moves only its key and those after it. Outputs are kept by content hash, never overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown, with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate passed and it is no worse than the one accepted before: no answer dropped, no measure moved the wrong way by a step a viewer could see, no gate missing. Later nodes read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps four actions, buttons in the cockpit: reject an accepted output (it moves back to the one before, and every node that read it is stale), accept a worse one with a reason, waive one gate for one key, and override the runner for one paid run. A barrier keeps agents from them: a store that refuses the creator's records from an agent's process, and a hook.

\textbf{Batches.} Paid work is bought as a batch, with one estimate and one ETA; the creator's yes to that estimate is the only spend decision, and the batch stops at its high estimate plus 10 percent. A scene enters a batch only once its design stands whole on its lock, and Buy waits until the film script is locked. Inside a batch every paid job runs once, with no retry; a failed output becomes a batch gap that the completion gate lists, and spending more on it is a new batch. The creator reviews each scene in a 3D viewer on its accepted assembly.

\subsection{From concept to scene}\label{from-concept-to-scene}

\textbf{The environment concept.} The creator first locks the EC, in a short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro \citep{gemini31pro} in the cockpit: its style and its \textbf{physics}, with his reference pictures. The EC holds the scenes and their layering: the top scenes and the child scenes each may hold. Its physics is what everything stands on and in and how it moves, at the scale he sets: a plot of ground with a gravity and a sun path, one world, or a system of bodies that move one another, a real body taking its values from a cited table and an invented one given by its radius, mass, orbit and spin. A body near enough to matter moves in the physics simulation, a far source of light is a light, and what is too far to move is a backdrop. An environment plan places each top scene's site on that ground, shaped by that scene's own plan, and works out the sky each site sees.

\textbf{A concept for each scene, top down.} For each scene a concept is then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene. Each draw is one Nano Banana Pro \citep{nanobananapro} call that the creator's click pays for, on a concept review page where he marks areas and asks for a new draw, or first discusses the draw with Gemini 3.1 Pro, the thread going with his next draw. A child scene is drawn only once its parent scene is locked and planned, on a crop of the parent's concept, so it fits the parent it stands in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} The creator's lock is the approval. Gemini 3.1 Pro lists every object of the locked concept as a row of the scene inventory, with its kind, count, anchor and size in metres, and SAM 3 \citep{carion2025sam3} boxes each row on the concept. A row counts when both find it, or when Gemini names one large object (at least 2 percent of the picture) that SAM 3 misses, boxed by Gemini's region; a row without metres is a gap. Nothing is made or placed that is not a row. The plan is made in two calls. Nano Banana Pro first draws the scene from straight above, from the locked concept, in the frame of the scene's own proportions; Gemini 3.1 Pro then writes the plan in metres from that drawing, the concept and the inventory, element by element: outline, walls, openings, levels, stairs, roofs, terrain heights, each item with its facing, people as the spots they stand on, and the child scenes' rooms. Free checks follow (every row placed as often as it counts, inside the outline, no two floor items in each other, each item placed and facing as in the concept, every door and standing spot reached on foot, the child scenes tiling their parent). What code can settle is fixed before the plan is shown, what remains goes back to Gemini 3.1 Pro as notes at most twice, and only what is left is a gap. A plan the creator drew himself wins over a written one, and his edits on the plan review page always win and redo what follows: he moves and resizes rooms, and a box he drops wholly inside a room is a thing in it, one dropped over another room makes the two one shared room, and one dropped anywhere else is a new child scene. A child scene he has locked keeps its room when its parent's plan is written again. Only a scene with a design gets the rows of a batch.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route is one loop, the same at every scale. A parent scene gets its concept, its plan and a rough model, its concept put through picture to 3D whole, which gives the plan its proportions. Its plan is then split into the shapes of its child scenes, and once those shapes are fixed every child scene is made in parallel, each repeating the loop (the creator's order of 19 October 2026). The runner's rows \texttt{rough-model} and \texttt{shell-split} (gated on \texttt{tiled}, \texttt{reached} and \texttt{bounded}) are built and have not run.

\textbf{The scene record.} The scene record holds what no made piece covers (shells, stairs, ground and water, backdrop, lamps, the neighbours a scene shows), written from the plan, inventory and made ground, and gated on \texttt{game-free}, \texttt{plan-matched} (the shell within 5 cm of the plan) and \texttt{openings} (every doorway onto a floor, no view into nothing).

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every scene is one OpenUSD stage \citep{openusd}; each object, one per real-world object, carries its kind, transform, collision and each part's library surface. Its layers are read off its design alone: each inventory row's kind, found free from the reading's noun, says what its objects do (emit, move, lie loose, glow, grow, act, sound), a scene gets an effects, motion, physics, crops or people layer only for rows that do so, and light and sound always. Each layer is made from the rows by their kind's rule, never from a record written for one scene, so a new environment needs no hand-written list. The layers stack in a fixed order: a slot for the creator's edit layer on top, then people, sound, light, motion, effects, crops, machines, settle, ground and the base, laid from the locked plan. Each scene is assembled once from every layer's accepted output; its \texttt{layers} gate answers unknown while a layer its design shows has none. Walls, floors, stairs and ground collide and every model has a collider (\texttt{collision}); colliders are static, mass and interaction not yet written. \textbf{A parent scene holds its child scenes}: its scene tree references each child's own tree at the room its plan gives it, so opening a top scene opens all below it, each still its own layer stack (\texttt{child\ scenes}). Neighbours are referenced the same way; a shown scene no stage makes is a gap. The edit layer is still a slot: a test on a two-box scene shows an edit there surviving a regenerated base, but the assembly does not yet carry the creator's edits from one assembly to the next.

\textbf{Models, generation first.} Every object goes the prop route first: Nano Banana Pro draws its close-up once from its crop of the concept, Pixal3D \citep{li2026pixal3d} builds it, the surface stage paints it, and a likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with its close-up. A close-up that fails the shape check is a batch gap, so Pixal3D is never paid for it. Only an object whose checks fail is built by a code builder a coding agent writes (the creator's choice, 12 October 2026). A big object the viewer walks up to gets a body built in code under the generated details its entry names (the creator, 18 October 2026).
