\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

An environment is a table of stages; environment 1's has 149 rows, 89 of them one for each person's look, dress or take. A \textbf{node} is one stage for one scene (\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the environment, and one runner makes, judges and accepts every node. The creator works in the creator cockpit, one signed-in web page: there he designs each scene (Section 2.2), buys paid work as batches and reviews what the runner reports. From an approved design everything runs without a further yes.

\subsection{Keys, gates and accepts}\label{keys-gates-and-accepts}

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record. The key hashes the stage and scene, a code version, the contracts of its output and inputs, its parameters, a seed derived from the environment's, the pinned model images, every environment file it reads and the accepted output of every node it reads. The code version hashes every source file the stage imports, leaving out the runner and the renting code, which change what a run costs, not what it makes. A fault fixed in one stage moves only its key and those after it. Outputs are kept by content hash, never overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown, with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate passed and it is no worse than the one accepted before: no answer dropped, no measure moved the wrong way by a step a viewer could see, no gate missing. Later nodes read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps four actions, buttons in the cockpit applied on his own PC: reject an accepted output (it moves back to the one before, and every node that read it is stale), accept a worse one with a reason, waive one gate for one key, and override the runner for one paid run. When the creator accepts a worse output, that accept records what was allowed, and a later output no worse than the one taken is accepted with that accept named. None is needed for a run; a barrier keeps agents from them (a store that refuses the creator's records from an agent's process, and a hook).

\textbf{Batches.} Paid work is bought as a batch. Before it starts, the runner gives one estimate and one ETA from measured unit costs (Section 3.7); the creator's yes to that estimate is the only spend decision, and the batch's stop is its own high estimate plus 10 percent. Inside a batch every paid job runs once, with no retry; its checks only sort each output into a success or a named failure, and a failure becomes a batch gap that the line carries to the completion gate instead of stopping. Spending more on a gap is a new batch and a new yes. A paid node is cleared only when every gate on its inputs passed, the month's books are known from the providers' bills, and its batch's stop has room for its forecast; everything that rents a machine or calls a paid model refuses without that clearance, and buying again more than EUR 5 of accepted outputs needs a written reason. Each node's review page is built from the store alone, and the creator reviews a scene in a 3D viewer on the store's copy of its accepted assembly.

\textbf{The creator steers: the ship.} Figure 1 shows the creator's control on one piece of environment 1, the ship, in its first run. The creator's picks were found pictures of a shuttle, a one-deck cutaway, a cockpit and crew quarters; we do not show them. From them an agent planned the bridge alone (v1, a 4.6 by 4.6 m room of 27 pieces). The creator corrected the plan in words: a smaller cockpit, the rooms environment 1 needs, a long flat wedge rather than a rocket, a nose less pointed, and an outline measured from drawings rather than shaped from words. Each correction made a new plan, and the creator accepted the seventh. Every plan is ours and cost nothing; the two drawings the outline was traced from cost EUR 0.28 and are not shown. The ship's shape follows found artwork the creator chose to use: shuttle concept by PAUL (zana), found online.

\subsection{From concept to scene}\label{from-concept-to-scene}

\textbf{The design.} Before any paid batch the creator decides each scene's design: its concept and its plan. He first locks an environment concept (EC), in a short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro \citep{gemini31pro} in the cockpit: its style and its \textbf{physics}, with his reference pictures. The EC holds the scenes and their layering, its top scenes and the child scenes each may hold. Its physics is what everything stands on and in and how it moves, at the scale he sets: a plot of ground with a gravity and a sun path, one world, or a system of bodies that move one another, a real body taking its values from a cited table and an invented one given by its radius, mass, orbit and spin. Each thing in it takes the form its scale calls for: a body near enough to matter moves in the physics simulation, a far source of light is a light, and what is too far to move is a backdrop. An environment plan, written by the plan writer below, places each top scene's site on its physics' ground at true size and works out each site's sky at its scene time. For each scene a concept is then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene, drawn on a concept review page, each draw one Nano Banana Pro \citep{nanobananapro} call that the creator's click pays for, where he marks areas and asks for a new draw. Every new draw is sent a \textbf{blockout} first: a grey model built in code from its parent's plan at real sizes and rendered free from the fixed concept view, so the draw keeps its layout and proportions and adds the look. A child scene is drawn only once its parent scene is picked and planned, on a crop of the parent scene's concept and its own shell inside the parent's, so child scenes fit the parent scene they stand in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} The creator's pick is the approval. It starts the scene inventory reading: Gemini 3.1 Pro lists every object of the picked concept as a row with its kind, count, anchor and size in metres, judged against the scene's outline, and SAM 3 \citep{carion2025sam3} boxes each row on the concept; a row without metres, or a disagreement between the two, is a named gap. One more Gemini 3.1 Pro call writes the plan in metres, element by element, as environment 1's hand-written plans were: outline, walls, openings with their swing, levels, stairs, roofs, terrain heights, each item's shape and a section. The SAM 3 boxes only order the items, since boxes from a perspective painting laid flat put objects in the wrong place. Free checks follow, each failure a gap said where it is: every row placed as often as it counts, inside the outline, no two floor items in each other, every door and standing spot reached on foot, and the child scenes tiling their parent scene. On the plan review page the creator moves, resizes and corrects, and his edits redo what follows. Readings and plan writings run without a yes under the creator's standing allowance, their cost shown in the cockpit. Only a scene with a design gets the rows of a batch.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route is one loop, the same at every scale. A scene starts as its big shape, the parent scene, with a concept, a plan and a rough model: the concept goes through picture to model whole, and the rough model gives the plan its proportions. The parent scene's plan is then split into the shapes of its child scenes (rooms, corridors, holds), each sized inside it. Once those shapes are fixed, every scene's concept, rough model, close-ups and finishes are made in parallel, since from then on each is a matter of proportions inside a fixed shape; zooming into a child scene repeats the same steps. This is the creator's order of 19 October 2026. The runner has its two rows: \texttt{rough-model} puts a scene's concept through Pixal3D whole after its plan, and \texttt{shell-split}, free, cuts a parent scene's plan into its child scenes' shapes, gated on \texttt{tiled}, \texttt{reached} and \texttt{bounded}, which each child scene's plan reads. Neither has run on environment 1: no rough model has been made, and no plan of it has a split yet.

\textbf{Plan and scene record.} Nothing is made or placed that is not an inventory row. The plan stage is gated on \texttt{mapped} (every element a row, a door or a written drop; every door says where it leads). The scene record is written from the plan, inventory, made ground and sky, gated on \texttt{game-free} (the record cites nothing from the game the environment came from), \texttt{plan-matched} (the shell within 5 cm of the plan), \texttt{openings} (every doorway onto a floor, no view into nothing) and \texttt{seed-matched} (every old entry matched or dropped with a reason). The \texttt{game-free} gate sees cited numbers only. In environment 1's first run a coding agent wrote the inventories and plans, and in 18 of its scenes the data still took positions from the source game (Section 4).

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every scene is one OpenUSD stage \citep{openusd}; each object, one per real-world object, carries its kind, transform, collision and each part's library surface. Its layers lie in a fixed order: a slot for the creator's edit layer on top, then people, sound, light, motion, effects, crops, machines, settle, ground and the generated base. The edit layer exists only as that slot, an empty layer the exporter makes once and never writes again; a test on a two-box scene shows an edit there surviving a regenerated base, but the runner's assembly does not yet carry a creator's edit from one assembly to the next. Each scene is assembled once, from every layer's accepted output; its \texttt{layers} gate answers unknown while a needed layer has none, and an old output never stands in.

\textbf{Models, generation first.} Every object goes the prop route first: Nano Banana Pro draws its close-up once from its crop of the concept, Pixal3D \citep{li2026pixal3d} builds it, the surface stage paints it, and a likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with its close-up. A close-up that fails the shape check's measured half (a plain border, one object filling the frame, the row's proportions) is a batch gap, so Pixal3D is never paid for it. Only an object whose checks fail is built by a code builder a coding agent writes, reversing the earlier parts check (the creator's choice, 12 October 2026). On 13 of the hub's objects both orders end the same (Table 1, Figure 2), but generation first draws a close-up of every object. Every close-up of eight thin and flat objects failed the shape check, so code builds carry them. The hatch wheel shows the gain: its code build's outline overlap was 0.45, a fail, and its generated model's 0.60.

\textbf{Big objects seen up close.} A generated model is right in its details and lumpy in its main shapes, which shows on a big object the viewer walks up to, so such an object gets a body built in code under only the generated details its entry names (a console's screens and lamps), both baked as one model in library surfaces (the creator, 18 October 2026). A re-audit of environment 1's 479 code-built inventory rows kept 396 as code, sent 80 to the prop route and 3 to a code body with generated details, and listed 46 picture-made kinds that are big objects seen up close; eight have a body builder (a cockpit console, a station module, a power core, fuel and water tanks, a lander, a rover and a crane). A prop whose body has no builder stops before its first paid step. The trial of the bodied props has not run.
