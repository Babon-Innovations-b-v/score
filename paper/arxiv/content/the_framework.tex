\section{The framework}\label{the-framework}

The creator works in the creator cockpit, one signed-in web page: there he decides the design, buys paid work and reviews what the framework reports. Below him, an environment is a table of stages; environment 1's has 149 rows. A \textbf{node} is one stage for one scene (\texttt{assembly@hangar}) or for the environment, and one runner makes, judges and accepts every node. From an approved design everything runs without a further yes.

\subsection{Keys, gates and accepts}\label{keys-gates-and-accepts}

\textbf{Keys.} A node is made only when its key has no finish record. The key hashes the stage and scene, a code version, the contracts of its output and inputs, its parameters, a seed derived from the environment's, the pinned model images, every environment file it reads and the accepted output of every node it reads. A fault fixed in one stage moves only its key and those after it. Outputs are kept by content hash, never overwritten.

\textbf{Gates and accepts.} Each gate answers pass, fail or unknown, with measures and a reason; missing evidence is unknown, and unknown blocks like fail. The runner accepts an output by itself when every gate passed and it is no worse than the one accepted before: no answer dropped, no measure moved the wrong way by a step a viewer could see, no gate missing. Later nodes read only accepted outputs. The creator keeps four actions, buttons in the cockpit: reject an accepted output (it moves back to the one before, and every node that read it is stale), accept a worse one with a reason, waive one gate for one key, and override the runner for one paid run. A barrier keeps agents from them: a store that refuses the creator's records from an agent's process, and a hook.

\textbf{Batches.} Paid work is bought as a batch, with one estimate and one ETA; the creator's yes to that estimate is the only spend decision, and the batch stops at its high estimate plus 10 percent. Inside a batch every paid job runs once, with no retry; a failed output becomes a batch gap that the completion gate lists, and spending more on it is a new batch. Each node's review page is built from the store alone, and the creator reviews a scene in a 3D viewer on the store's copy of its accepted assembly.

\subsection{From concept to scene}\label{from-concept-to-scene}

\textbf{The environment concept.} The creator first locks the EC, in a short interview with Gemini 3.1 Pro \citep{gemini31pro} in the cockpit: its style and its \textbf{physics}, with his reference pictures. The EC holds the scenes and their layering: the top scenes and the child scenes each may hold. Its physics is what everything stands on and in and how it moves, at the scale he sets: a plot of ground with a gravity and a sun path, one world, or a system of bodies that move one another, a real body taking its values from a cited table and an invented one given by its radius, mass, orbit and spin. A body near enough to matter moves in the physics simulation, a far source of light is a light, and what is too far to move is a backdrop. An environment plan places each top scene's site on that ground at true size and works out the sky each site sees.

\textbf{A concept for each scene, top down.} For each scene a concept is then locked, in order of parent scene and child scene. Each draw is one Nano Banana Pro \citep{nanobananapro} call that the creator's click pays for, on a concept review page where he marks areas and asks for a new draw. Every draw is sent a \textbf{blockout} first, a grey model built in code from the parent's plan at real sizes, so it keeps that layout and proportions and adds the look. A child scene is drawn only once its parent scene is locked and planned, on a crop of the parent's concept, so it fits the parent it stands in.

\textbf{Inventory and plan.} The creator's lock is the approval. Gemini 3.1 Pro lists every object of the locked concept as a row of the scene inventory, with its kind, count, anchor and size in metres, and SAM 3 \citep{carion2025sam3} boxes each row on the concept; a row without metres, or a disagreement between the two, is a gap. Nothing is made or placed that is not a row. One more Gemini 3.1 Pro call writes the plan in metres, element by element: outline, walls, openings, levels, stairs, roofs, terrain heights and each item. Free checks follow: every row placed as often as it counts, inside the outline, no two floor items in each other, every door and standing spot reached on foot, and the child scenes tiling their parent. On the plan review page the creator corrects the plan, and his edits redo what follows. Only a scene with a design gets the rows of a batch.

\textbf{Shapes first.} The route is one loop, the same at every scale. A parent scene gets its concept, its plan and a rough model, its concept put through picture to 3D whole, which gives the plan its proportions. Its plan is then split into the shapes of its child scenes, and once those shapes are fixed every child scene is made in parallel, each repeating the loop (the creator's order of 19 October 2026). The runner's rows \texttt{rough-model} and \texttt{shell-split} (gated on \texttt{tiled}, \texttt{reached} and \texttt{bounded}) are built and have not run.

\textbf{The scene record.} The scene record holds what no made piece covers (shells, stairs, ground and water, backdrop, lights, sky, the neighbours a scene shows), written from the plan, inventory, made ground and sky, and gated on \texttt{plan-matched} (the shell within 5 cm of the plan), \texttt{openings} (every doorway onto a floor, no view into nothing) and \texttt{seed-matched} (every old entry matched or dropped with a reason).

\textbf{The canonical scene.} Every scene is one OpenUSD stage \citep{openusd}; each object, one per real-world object, carries its kind, transform, collision and each part's library surface. Each department's work is a layer, in a fixed order: a slot for the creator's edit layer on top, then people, sound, light, motion, effects, crops, machines, settle, ground and the generated base. Each scene is assembled once, from every layer's accepted output; its \texttt{layers} gate answers unknown while a needed layer has none, and an old output never stands in. A scene shows its neighbours by reference to their own accepted assemblies. The edit layer is still a slot: a test on a two-box scene shows an edit there surviving a regenerated base, but the assembly does not yet carry the creator's edits from one assembly to the next.

\textbf{Models, generation first.} Every object goes the prop route first: Nano Banana Pro draws its close-up once from its crop of the concept, Pixal3D \citep{li2026pixal3d} builds it, the surface stage paints it, and a likeness gate compares its outline from eight sides with its close-up. A close-up that fails the shape check is a batch gap, so Pixal3D is never paid for it. Only an object whose checks fail is built by a code builder a coding agent writes (the creator's choice, 12 October 2026). A big object the viewer walks up to gets a body built in code under the generated details its entry names (the creator, 18 October 2026).
